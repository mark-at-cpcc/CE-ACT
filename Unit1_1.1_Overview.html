Welcome to Week 1. By Thursday afternoon you will be able to open a computer, name every major component inside it, and explain what each one does. That is not a metaphor. That is the objective.

Nothing Is Due Before You Arrive

Tuesday 8/11 is the first day of class. There is no preparation to do beforehand — you should have completed all pre-work with the ACT team. Your orientation is completed together in class on Tuesday, and assigned work begins after that first session. Just show up.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Identify the major components inside a desktop computer by sight and state the function of each
  2. Explain how the motherboard, processor, memory, and power supply depend on one another to boot a system
  3. Describe motherboard form factors, expansion slot types, and internal connector standards
  4. Explain the role of the BIOS/UEFI firmware and locate common configuration settings
  5. Apply proper electrostatic discharge (ESD) and electrical safety procedures during hardware service
  6. Apply CompTIA's six-step troubleshooting methodology to a no-boot scenario
Technical Overview

Unit 1 addresses CompTIA A+ Core 1 (220-1201) Domain 3.0 Hardware — specifically objectives 3.3 (memory), 3.5 (motherboards, CPUs, expansion cards, cooling), and 3.6 (power) — together with the opening portion of Domain 5.0 Hardware and Network Troubleshooting, objective 5.1. Instruction covers motherboard form factors and expansion bus architectures, processor characteristics and socket compatibility, volatile memory technologies and error-correction schemes, power supply specifications and connector standards, thermal management, and UEFI/BIOS firmware configuration including TPM. The unit establishes the structured troubleshooting methodology used as the diagnostic framework for the remainder of the course.

In Plain Terms

This week is about what is physically inside the box and how those parts talk to each other. The motherboard is the classroom. The processor is the brain. Memory is the desk you work on. The power supply feeds all of it. Cooling keeps it from cooking itself. Once you know the parts, you can start figuring out which one is broken — and that skill is more than a quarter of your exam.

Why We Start Here

Hardware plus hardware troubleshooting makes up 53% of the Core 1 exam. It is also the most concrete material in the entire course. You can hold a stick of RAM. You can hear a fan. You cannot hold a networking concept.

That matters more than it might sound. Starting with things you can physically touch gives you somewhere to attach the vocabulary that comes later. Students who begin with abstract networking concepts often report that the terms slide off. Students who begin with their hands in a case tend to remember, three weeks later, that a DIMM slot has a notch in it — because they cut their thumb on one.

No Experience Required

This course assumes you have never opened a computer. If you have, you will move faster through Tuesday. If you have not, you are exactly who this unit was written for. Nobody in this room started out knowing this.

See It Before You Name It

You begin with a survey of computer equipment. The case is opened, you will look inside, and identify what is there before we give any of it a name. That order is intentional.

Beginners handed a vocabulary list first have nothing to attach it to, and the terms slide off within days. Beginners who have already looked at a DIMM slot have somewhere to put the word "DIMM" when it arrives on Wednesday. You will not remember every term from this unit — but you will remember what the inside of a computer looks like, and that is the hook everything else hangs on.

How This Unit Is Graded

Every item in this unit is worth 100 points and is awarded in full when it meets the completion standard published on that item's page. Work that falls short is returned for revision and resubmitted for full credit — it is not partially scored.

That structure exists so you can stop calculating your grade and start focusing on the only score that decides anything: the scaled score on your practice exams. On the real 220-1201 you need 675 out of 900. Because the scale starts at 100 rather than zero, that works out to roughly 72% of raw points — not 75%. We will track that number every week.
