Three classroom labs, optional and run as time permits, and one take-home lab, expected. None of these are graded; the NETLAB labs in Section 1.8 are. Nothing in this unit is built with a crimper — cable work is identification and testing of made-up cables only.

1

Lab 1.6.1 — Layer Sort

Estimated Time: 30 minutes · Classroom · Objective 1.1

A deck of cards, each naming a protocol, a device, a PDU, or an address type. Sort them onto the seven OSI layers, then defend three placements to the group.

You Will Need

  • The printed card deck (instructor provides)
  • Seven layer labels taped down the table

Steps

  1. Sort in silence. Each team sorts the full deck without discussion — eight minutes.
  2. Compare. Teams walk the room and note where other teams placed cards differently.
  3. Defend three. Each team picks three contested cards and argues the layer, using the PDU or the address as evidence (“it has a port number, so it is Layer 4”).
  4. Fix the deck. The group agrees on every card; the instructor settles ties with the objectives document.

What to Record

The three cards you were surest about and got wrong. Those are your Layer 2/3 and Layer 4/7 boundary problems, and they will be on the unit test.

2

Lab 1.6.2 — Identify and Test

Estimated Time: 45 minutes · Classroom · Objectives 1.5, 5.2

A bin of made-up cables and connectors. Sort them by type, read the jacket print, then run each through the cable tester and read the result. One cable in the bin is faulty.

You Will Need

  • The cable bin: Cat 5e, Cat 6, Cat 6a, coaxial, a fiber patch cable, RJ11, RJ45, F-type, LC, SC, and ST ends
  • A cable tester (wire-map type)
  • No crimper — nothing is built in this lab

Steps

  1. Sort by medium — copper twisted pair, coaxial, fiber — and then by connector.
  2. Read the jacket. Every certified cable is printed with its category, rating, and often its length. Write down the category of each twisted-pair cable from the print, not from the color.
  3. Test each twisted-pair cable. Plug both ends into the tester and read the wire map. Record pass or fail and, on a fail, which pins.
  4. Find the bad one and say what is wrong with it — open pin, miswire, split pair — and what symptom a user would report.
  5. Fiber by eye only. Identify single-mode versus multimode by jacket color and connector polish (blue UPC, green APC). Never look into the end of a fiber that might be lit.

What to Record

A one-line entry per cable: medium, category or mode, connector, tester result. It is the inventory a technician makes on any site visit.

3

Lab 1.6.3 — Walk the Closet

Estimated Time: 30 minutes · Classroom · Objectives 1.6, 2.4

An observe-only visit to a real wiring closet (the instructor arranges access). You touch nothing; you read everything.

You Will Need

  • A notebook and a phone camera, if photography is permitted in the room

Steps

  1. Find the demarc or the uplink that leads toward it. Which side of the room is the carrier’s, and which is the college’s?
  2. Trace one horizontal run from a patch-panel port to a switch port. Note the labeling scheme on both.
  3. Count the switches, the patch panels, the fiber enclosures, and the UPS. Note what is labeled and what is not.
  4. Identify whether this room is an MDF or an IDF, and what tells you.

What to Record

A sketch of the room as a diagram: devices as boxes, cables as lines, with the labels you could read. Compare it with the three-tier diagram in Chapter 1 and say which tier this closet is.

H

Lab 1.6.4 — Take-Home: Map What You’re On

Estimated Time: 1 – 1.5 hours · At home · Objectives 1.6, 3.1, 5.5

Document your own home network the way a technician documents a client’s: a physical diagram, a logical diagram, and the facts behind them. It is the first ticket you write for yourself, and the baseline you will return to in Unit 2 (addressing) and Unit 4 (Wi-Fi).

Your Own Equipment Only

Run this lab only on the network you own or are explicitly authorized to use. Never run scanning, capture, or configuration changes against anything else.

Steps

  1. Walk it physically. Find the point where the provider’s cable enters (coax, fiber, or phone line), the modem or gateway, the router, any switch, and every wired device. Draw the physical diagram: boxes and lines, with the medium on each line.
  2. Read your own machine. Run ipconfig /all (Windows) or ifconfig / ip a (macOS, Linux). Record your MAC address, your IP address, the subnet mask, the default gateway, and the DNS servers. Do not interpret them yet — Unit 2 does that.
  3. Open the router’s status page (the address is usually the default gateway) and record the WAN address, the LAN address, and the list of connected devices.
  4. Draw the logical diagram: the same devices, but now with the addresses on them and the router drawn as the boundary between your LAN and the provider’s WAN.
  5. Name the topology you drew and the OSI layer each device works at.

What to Record

Write it up as a ticket: device, site, what you found, and two questions you could not answer. Bring it Tuesday; Unit 2’s first ticket starts from it.

T

Ticket of the Day

One ticket per class meeting, worked on your own

This unit’s three tickets live in Section 1.11 Tickets: The Link Light Is Dark (Tuesday), Slow Every Afternoon (Wednesday), and Four Hundred Meters (Thursday). Each takes about twenty minutes.

What These Labs Give You

Objectives 1.5 and 1.6 are “compare and contrast” objectives, and objective 1.1 is “explain.” The exam tests all three with pictures and scenarios: a photo of a connector, a diagram of a closet, a description of a symptom. The only way to recognize a thing in a picture is to have held it; the only way to explain a layer is to have argued about it. These labs are where the reading becomes something you can see.
