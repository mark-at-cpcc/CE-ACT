Welcome to the course. Before anyone configures a router or subnets an address, a technician has to know what a network physically is: the wire, the light, the connector on the end, and the seven-layer model that tells you where in the stack a problem lives. That is this unit.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Define what a network is and distinguish LAN, WAN, MAN, PAN, and WLAN by scope and purpose
  2. Compare peer-to-peer and client-server architectures and the bus, star, ring, mesh, point-to-point, point-to-multipoint, and hybrid topologies
  3. Describe the three-tier, collapsed-core, and spine-and-leaf designs and the traffic flows they are built for
  4. Name the seven OSI layers, state what travels at each, and trace encapsulation from data to bits and back
  5. Identify coaxial, twisted-pair, twinaxial, and fiber media and their connectors by inspection, and state the speed and distance limits of each category
  6. Match transceivers (SFP, SFP+, QSFP, QSFP+, QSFP28) and media converters to the media and speeds they serve
  7. Explain the T568A and T568B standards and when a straight-through, crossover, or rollover cable is used
  8. Explain how Ethernet puts bits on the wire — collision and broadcast domains, CSMA/CD, duplex, MAC addressing, and the frame
Technical Overview

Unit 1 addresses CompTIA Network+ (N10-009) Domain 1.0 Networking Concepts, objectives 1.1 (the OSI reference model), 1.5 (transmission media and transceivers), and 1.6 (network topologies, architectures, and types). Instruction covers network scope and architecture, physical and logical topologies, three-tier and spine-and-leaf designs and north-south versus east-west traffic flow, service-related entry points and provider links, virtual networking, the seven OSI layers and encapsulation, copper and fiber media and connectors, transceiver form factors, cable properties and wiring standards, MDF/IDF distribution, and the Ethernet specifications — collision and broadcast domains, CSMA/CD, duplex, MAC addressing, and frame structure.

In Plain Terms

A network is a set of devices that can talk to each other, and the first thing a technician learns is what the conversation runs on — copper, glass, or air — and what shape the connections make. The OSI model is the map every network person carries in their head: seven layers from the physical wire at the bottom to the application at the top. When a user says “the internet is down,” the technician who can say “this is a Layer 1 problem — the link light is dark” fixes it in two minutes. The one who starts rebooting things takes an hour. This unit gives you the map and the wire.

Why We Start Here

Networking is invisible until it fails, and the most common failures are physical: a bad patch cable, a wrong transceiver, a cable run past its distance limit, a port forced to half duplex. Starting with media and the OSI model gives you two things at once — the vocabulary for every later unit (“Layer 2,” “Layer 3”) and the first place to look when something breaks. Domain 1.0 is also the largest domain on the exam at 23%, and these three objectives are its foundation.

Every unit after this one assumes you can name the layer a thing lives at. Switches (Unit 3) are Layer 2 devices; routers are Layer 3; DHCP and DNS (Unit 2) are Layer 7 services riding on Layer 4 ports. Get the model solid now and the rest of the course has somewhere to hang.

CompTIA Network+ Passes at 720

The exam is scored on a 100–900 scale and passes at 720. Because the scale starts at 100 rather than zero, 720 works out to about 78% of raw points. That is a high bar: a student who treats 70% on a practice set as “good enough” fails the real exam. This course’s mastery gate of 800 exists so that your exam day has a cushion.
