What a Network Is

Technical Overview

A network is two or more devices connected so they can share data and resources. Networks are classified by scope: a PAN (personal area network) spans a few meters around one person; a LAN (local area network) spans a room, floor, or building under one organization’s control; a WLAN is a LAN carried over 802.11 wireless; a CAN (campus) joins the LANs of adjacent buildings; a MAN (metropolitan) spans a city; a WAN (wide area network) spans regions or the world and usually rides on a provider’s links. A SAN (storage area network) is a dedicated network for block storage. Common components are hosts, network interface cards, switches, routers, access points, servers, and the media between them.

In Plain Terms

Scope is just “how far does it reach, and who owns it.” Your phone and earbuds are a PAN. Your office floor is a LAN. The two buildings across the parking lot joined by a fiber run are a CAN. The link from the office to the data center across town is a WAN, and you rent it from a carrier. The exam wants the labels and, more often, the reasoning: a scenario describes a reach and asks which network type it is.

Peer-to-peer vs. client-server

In a peer-to-peer network every host both offers and consumes resources, with no central authority — simple, cheap, and unmanageable past a handful of machines. In a client-server network dedicated servers provide services (files, authentication, mail, web) and clients consume them — centralized control, centralized security, and the model every business network uses.

On the Job

A new client with eight staff asks why their shared files keep disappearing. Each PC shares a folder; whoever turns a machine off takes its files with it. That is peer-to-peer failing at the size it always fails at. The fix is a server — and the exam question that describes the same symptoms wants you to say “client-server.”

Topologies

Technical Overview

A topology is the arrangement of connections. A physical topology is how the cables actually run; a logical topology is how data actually flows, and the two can differ. Bus: one shared cable, terminated at both ends; a single break takes down the segment. Star (hub-and-spoke): every host cabled to a central device; the design of nearly every modern LAN. Ring: each device connected to the next in a loop; a break stops the ring unless it is dual. Mesh: devices connected to many or all others — full mesh needs n(n−1)/2 links and is used for WAN cores and small critical sites; partial mesh is the practical compromise. Point-to-point: one link between two devices (a WAN circuit, a wireless bridge). Point-to-multipoint: one central node reaching several endpoints (an access point and its clients). Hybrid: any combination, which is what real networks are.

The exam asks about topology by consequence

“Which topology lets one cable fault isolate a single workstation?” is a star question. “Which topology requires the most cabling?” is full mesh. Learn each one as a cause-and-effect pair — what it costs and what it survives — and the picture questions answer themselves.

Architectures, Backbones, and Where the Provider Hands Off

Technical Overview

The three-tier design divides a network into a core (fast, redundant, no policy), a distribution layer (routing, policy, aggregation), and an access layer (the switches hosts plug into). A collapsed core merges core and distribution for smaller sites. The spine-and-leaf design, used in data centers, connects every leaf (top-of-rack) switch to every spine switch so that any two servers are the same number of hops apart; it is built for east-west traffic (server to server) rather than the north-south traffic (client to data center and out) that three-tier optimizes. A backbone is the high-capacity path that joins segments; a segment is a portion of the network bounded by a device. The provider’s circuit ends at the demarcation point (demarc), often on a smartjack or a CSU/DSU; everything from there inward is yours. Provider links include fiber, cable, DSL, leased lines, cellular, and satellite. Virtual networking reproduces NICs and switches in software (vNIC, vSwitch) inside a hypervisor.

In Plain Terms

Three-tier is a tree: hosts at the leaves, a trunk in the middle, and policy applied on the way up. Spine-and-leaf is a grid: nothing is more than two hops from anything else, which is what you want when servers spend all day talking to each other. The demarc is the line on the floor where “call the carrier” becomes “that one’s ours.” Knowing which side of that line a fault is on is half of troubleshooting a WAN outage.

Where Students Trip

North-south and east-west are directions on the diagram, not compass points. Draw the data center with the servers side by side at the bottom and the internet at the top: client traffic runs up and down (north-south); server-to-server traffic runs sideways (east-west). Spine-and-leaf exists because modern applications generate far more sideways traffic than up-and-down.

The OSI Reference Model

Technical Overview

The Open Systems Interconnection model divides network communication into seven layers. From the top: 7 Application (the interface to network services — HTTP, DNS, SMTP), 6 Presentation (data format, encryption, compression), 5 Session (establishing, maintaining, and ending dialogs), 4 Transport (end-to-end delivery — TCP segments, UDP datagrams, ports), 3 Network (logical addressing and path selection — IP packets, routers), 2 Data Link (physical addressing and framing — MAC addresses, switches, frames), 1 Physical (bits on the medium — cables, connectors, hubs, signaling). Encapsulation wraps data in a header (and at Layer 2 a trailer) at each layer on the way down; decapsulation unwraps it on the way up. The protocol data units are data (5–7), segment or datagram (4), packet (3), frame (2), and bits (1).

The model is a troubleshooting ladder

Every network problem lives at a layer, and the layer tells you the tool. No link light — Layer 1, check the cable and port. Link but no traffic to the neighbor — Layer 2, check the VLAN and the MAC table. Can reach the neighbor but not the far network — Layer 3, check the address, mask, and gateway. Can reach the server but not the service — Layer 4, check the port and the firewall. Learn the layers as the order you check things in, and you will have learned CompTIA’s troubleshooting method before Unit 6 teaches it.

A Mnemonic That Works

Top down: All People Seem To Need Data Processing. Bottom up: Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away. Pick one and say the layer numbers with it; the exam asks “Layer 3” as often as it says “Network layer.”

Modulation, briefly

Layer 1 has to turn bits into something a medium can carry — a voltage on copper, a pulse of light in glass, a radio wave in air. Modulation is the method: varying amplitude, frequency, or phase of a carrier to represent the data. You will not be asked to compute it; you will be asked to recognize that bit rate (bits per second) and baud (signal changes per second) are different, because one signal change can carry more than one bit.

Transmission Media

Technical Overview

Coaxial cable (RG-6 for cable TV and cable modems; RG-59 for short, low-frequency runs) carries a signal on a center conductor inside a shield; connectors are F-type (threaded) and BNC (bayonet). Twisted-pair cable has four pairs, twisted to cancel interference; UTP is unshielded, STP is shielded, and the category sets the bandwidth: Cat 5e (1 Gbps, 100 m), Cat 6 (1 Gbps to 100 m; 10 Gbps to 55 m), Cat 6a (10 Gbps, 100 m), Cat 7 and Cat 8 (shielded, 25–40 Gbps at 30 m, data-center use). Connectors are RJ45 (8P8C, Ethernet) and RJ11 (telephone). Twinaxial cable is a short, shielded two-conductor cable used in direct attach copper (DAC) assemblies with transceivers permanently attached, for runs of a few meters in a rack. Fiber carries light: single-mode (SMF, 9 µm core, laser, kilometers) and multimode (MMF, 50 or 62.5 µm core, LED or VCSEL, hundreds of meters). Fiber connectors are LC (small, most common on transceivers), SC (push-pull), ST (bayonet, older), and MPO/MTP (multi-fiber, 40/100 Gbps). Polish matters: UPC (blue, flat) and APC (green, angled) do not mate.

Transceivers

A transceiver is the pluggable module that converts a switch or router port to a specific medium and speed. SFP (1 Gbps), SFP+ (10 Gbps), SFP28 (25 Gbps), QSFP+ (40 Gbps), and QSFP28 (100 Gbps) are the form factors you must know; the older GBIC did the same job in a larger package. Each module is built for one medium: 1000BASE-SX for multimode, 1000BASE-LX for single-mode, 10GBASE-SR, 10GBASE-LR, and so on. A media converter does the same translation as a standalone box — copper to fiber, multimode to single-mode — where a device has no pluggable port.

Cable properties

Every medium is rated for transmission speed, distance, duplex (half, one direction at a time; full, both at once), noise immunity (resistance to EMI and crosstalk — shielding and fiber win), and frequency (the bandwidth in MHz that sets the category). Exceed the distance and the signal attenuates below what the receiver can read; run UTP beside a fluorescent ballast or a motor and EMI corrupts it. Fiber is immune to both EMI and lightning-induced ground differences, which is why building-to-building runs are fiber.

On the Job

A customer’s new 10-gigabit camera server “only gets 1 gig.” The run is 80 meters of Cat 6. Nothing is broken — Cat 6 carries 10GBASE-T only to 55 meters, so the link negotiated down. Replacing the run with Cat 6a fixes it. That is objective 1.5 and objective 5.2 in one call, and it is the kind of scenario the exam loves.

Wiring Standards and the Wiring Closet

Technical Overview

TIA/EIA-568 defines two pinouts for RJ45: T568A and T568B, which differ only in swapping the green and orange pairs. A straight-through cable has the same standard on both ends and connects unlike devices (host to switch). A crossover cable has A on one end and B on the other, crossing the transmit and receive pairs to connect like devices (switch to switch, host to host) — largely unnecessary now that ports auto-sense (Auto-MDI-X), but still on the exam. A rollover (console) cable reverses all eight pins and connects a serial console port to a terminal; it is not an Ethernet cable at all. Cabling from the provider’s demarc enters the MDF (main distribution frame), the building’s primary wiring closet, and runs to IDFs (intermediate distribution frames) on each floor or wing; horizontal runs terminate on patch panels via punchdown blocks (110 for data; 66 for legacy voice), and patch cables jump from panel to switch.

Where Students Trip

Pins 1–2 and 3–6 are the transmit and receive pairs for 10/100 Ethernet; gigabit uses all four pairs. A cable with pins 3 and 6 on different pairs (a “split pair”) can pass a simple continuity test and still fail at speed, because the twist that cancels noise is gone. This is why a cable tester that checks wire map and a certifier that checks performance are different tools (Unit 6).

Ethernet: How Bits Actually Get on the Wire

Technical Overview

Ethernet (IEEE 802.3) is the Layer 1 and Layer 2 standard for wired LANs. A collision domain is the set of devices whose frames can collide — everything on a hub or a shared cable; a switch puts each port in its own collision domain. A broadcast domain is the set of devices that receive each other’s broadcasts — a whole switched LAN or VLAN; only a router (or Layer 3 switch) bounds it. CSMA/CD (carrier sense multiple access with collision detection) is how half-duplex Ethernet shares a collision domain: listen, transmit, detect a collision, back off a random interval, retry. Full duplex transmits and receives simultaneously on separate pairs with no collisions at all, which is why modern switched Ethernet effectively retired CSMA/CD. Baseband uses the whole medium for one signal (Ethernet); broadband divides it into channels (cable TV). Every NIC has a 48-bit MAC address, written in hexadecimal, whose first 24 bits are the manufacturer’s OUI. An Ethernet frame carries preamble, destination MAC, source MAC, type/length, payload (46–1500 bytes), and a frame check sequence (CRC) that lets the receiver detect corruption.

Hubs versus switches is the whole story

A hub repeats every bit out every port: one collision domain, one broadcast domain, half duplex, CSMA/CD doing the best it can. A switch reads the destination MAC and forwards only to the right port: one collision domain per port, full duplex, no collisions. Both are still one broadcast domain until a router divides it. If you can explain that paragraph without notes, you understand Layer 2.

Binary, decimal, and hexadecimal

MAC addresses are written in hex (3C-52-82-4B-91-0A); IP addresses are written in decimal but computed in binary. A hex digit is four bits; a byte is two hex digits or a decimal number from 0 to 255. Unit 2 builds the binary skills that subnetting needs; this unit only asks you to read hex and know that FF is 255 and 11111111.

Beyond the Exam
