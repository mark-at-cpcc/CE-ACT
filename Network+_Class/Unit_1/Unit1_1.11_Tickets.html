Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three trouble tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you?
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them yourself.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution. Answer them in your notes.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of output from the network being troubleshot — what you would see on a screen, read on a label, or hold in your hand. They are simplified to show what matters, the way a performance-based question on the N10-009 exam presents a tool’s output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA’s troubleshooting methodology it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and identify potential effects
  5. Implement the solution or escalate as necessary
  6. Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures if applicable
  7. Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned throughout the process

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: new workstation BL-WS-14 has no network connectivity at cubicle 2E; the previous workstation at the same jack worked.

Found: ipconfig shows Media disconnected and the NIC has no link light — a Layer 1 condition. The patch cable supplied with the PC is Cat 3 telephone-grade cable; it passes a continuity test because all eight pins are connected, but Cat 3 is not rated for 100BASE-TX or 1000BASE-T and the gigabit NIC would not establish link. The wall jack and switch port 17 were not changed.

Action: replaced the patch cable with a Cat 5e cable from stock; link light came up, ipconfig obtained an address; confirmed the user could reach the file server and the internet.

Verified: switch port 17 shows 1000 Mbps full duplex; tested a second Cat 5e cable to rule out a one-off.

Follow-up: tagged the Cat 3 cable and removed it from the PC supply box; noted in the ticket that the “cable from the box” is not a network cable so the next technician does not repeat the hour.

Look Back

  1. What did you expect the cause to be before you saw Exhibit A, and what in the exhibit confirmed or overturned it?
  2. The tester said PASS and the cable was still the problem. In one sentence, what does a wire-map tester actually test?
  3. Dana said the jack was fine because someone told her so. How would you confirm a jack without taking anyone’s word for it?

RequesterMarcus Ellison, Studio Lead, Pinehill Architects (22 staff)
Site / DeviceDesign studio, 3rd floor · six drafting workstations, cubicle row C
SeverityMedium — six users; work continues slowly
CategoryPerformance — wired

The Call

“Row C is a crawl every afternoon when everyone is saving drawings. The rest of the floor is fine. Someone added a little box under the desk last year when we ran out of ports and it’s been like this ever since, but it got worse when we moved to the bigger drawing files.”

GigabitEthernet0/10 is up, line protocol is up
  Full-duplex, 1000Mb/s, media type is 10/100/1000BaseTX
  5 minute input rate 9,200,000 bits/sec

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: six workstations in row C slow every afternoon; rest of the floor unaffected.

Found: the device added under desk C1 is a 10/100 hub. A hub is a Layer 1 repeater: all six devices share one collision domain and one half-duplex 100 Mbps path, so the uplink on the switch runs half duplex and logs collisions and late collisions (Exhibit B). Row B, on its own switch port, runs 1000 Mbps full duplex with zero errors (Exhibit C). The larger drawing files made the shared segment saturate.

Action: replaced the hub with an 8-port gigabit switch; each workstation now has its own collision domain at 1000 Mbps full duplex, and the uplink negotiated to full duplex.

Verified: cleared counters on port 9 and watched through the afternoon save window: 0 collisions; users confirmed the slowdown was gone.

Follow-up: added the switch to the inventory and the floor diagram; opened a request to add wall jacks to row C so the desk switch is temporary rather than permanent.

Look Back

  1. Exhibit B said “Half-duplex” before it said “collisions.” Which one did you notice first, and which one explains the other?
  2. Marcus thinks the bigger files caused it. In one sentence, explain to him why the files revealed the problem rather than causing it.
  3. What does comparing port 9 with port 10 give you that looking at port 9 alone does not?

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: a contractor proposes a 400 m Cat 6a run for a 10 Gbps building-to-building link.

Found: 10GBASE-T over Cat 6a is limited to 100 m; a 400 m copper run cannot carry Ethernet at any speed and would also carry lightning and ground-potential differences between buildings into the switches. The box already contains 10GBASE-LR SFP+ modules, which are built for single-mode fiber to 10 km. OM4 multimode with 10GBASE-SR would reach exactly 400 m with no margin; single-mode is the correct choice for the distance.

Action: recommended to the board that the proposal be revised to single-mode (OS2) fiber, LC-terminated at both ends, using the 10GBASE-LR SFP+ modules on hand; asked the contractor for a revised quote with fiber and a fiber enclosure in each MDF.

Verified: confirmed the switches in both buildings have SFP+ slots; confirmed the LR modules are the switch vendor’s supported part.

Follow-up: added the fiber run, the modules, and the enclosures to the network diagram; noted the 1000BASE-T copper SFPs in inventory for future short runs.

Look Back

  1. The contractor’s note said “supports 10 gig.” It was true and still wrong. What word was missing?
  2. If the only modules in the box had been 10GBASE-SR, what would you have recommended, and what margin would it leave?
  3. Priya needs one sentence for the board. Write it, with no acronyms.
