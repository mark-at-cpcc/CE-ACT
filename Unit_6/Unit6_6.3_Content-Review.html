Technical Overview

This section consolidates the troubleshooting content distributed across Units 1 through 5 and organizes it around the CompTIA methodology. It covers the six-step process, reading a symptom across domain boundaries, and consolidated diagnostics for hardware, output devices, mobile devices, and networking — ending with how to choose between two competing diagnoses on the evidence available.

In Plain Terms

Up to now, every symptom you saw came with a label attached, because you saw it in the week you were studying that component. A drive problem showed up in drive week. That is a fine way to learn, and it is nothing like the exam or the job. Out there, a person says “it’s slow” and that one word could mean a failing drive, a saturated network link, a thermal-throttling processor, or an overloaded virtual machine. This section is about getting from “it’s slow” to the right domain before you start replacing things.

The Troubleshooting Methodology

Everything in this unit hangs on this process. Learn the order, not just the list — on the exam you will be given a technician who is partway through the method and asked what comes next, and the wrong answers are always real steps placed out of sequence.

StepWhat You DoWhat It Looks Like on the Job
1. Identify the problem Gather information from the user, identify user changes, and perform backups before making changes. Inquire regarding environmental or infrastructure changes. You ask what changed. Almost nothing breaks on its own; something moved, updated, got unplugged, or got rained on. You also back up data before you start, not after you break something.
2. Establish a theory of probable cause Question the obvious. Consider multiple approaches, including conducting external or internal research based on symptoms. You form a best guess. Start with the cheap, common, obvious causes before the exotic ones. A loose cable is more likely than a failed memory controller.
3. Test the theory to determine the cause Confirm the theory and determine the next steps to resolve the problem. If the theory is not confirmed, establish a new theory or escalate. You prove it or you drop it. A theory you never tested is still a guess, and guesses are how you end up replacing three good parts.
4. Establish a plan of action and implement the solution Refer to vendor documentation for guidance, then carry out the repair. You decide what you are going to do and then do it. Plan first so that the fix is one deliberate change rather than five hopeful ones you can no longer untangle.
5. Verify full system functionality Confirm the system works, and where applicable implement preventive measures. You check that the original complaint is gone and that you did not break something else on the way. Then you stop it from happening again if you can.
6. Document findings, actions, and outcomes Record the symptom, the cause, what you did, and the result. You write it down. The next person to see this symptom might be you in eight months, and you will not remember.

What Skipping a Step Actually Costs

The methodology looks like paperwork until you watch someone skip a step. Here is what each omission looks like in practice:

  • Skip Step 1 and you fix a problem the user did not have, because you never asked what they were actually seeing.
  • Skip Step 3 and you replace a part on a hunch. When the symptom persists, you now have two unknowns instead of one.
  • Skip Step 5 and the user calls back in an hour, because the fix solved the complaint and broke printing.
  • Skip Step 6 and the organization learns nothing. The same failure costs the same hours every time it recurs.

The exam tests Step 5 and Step 6 more heavily than most candidates expect, precisely because they are the two people skip in the field.

Back Up First

Backing up data before making changes sits inside Step 1 for a reason. Once you have started swapping parts or reconfiguring a system, the opportunity to preserve the user’s data may already be gone. When a question describes a technician about to work on a machine holding the only copy of something, the correct answer almost always involves protecting that data before anything else happens.

Reading a Symptom Across Domains

A single reported symptom rarely points at a single domain. The skill is knowing what evidence would separate the possibilities, then gathering exactly that evidence instead of guessing.

What the User SaysCould BeThe Evidence That Decides
“It’s really slow.” Failing drive (3.0) · Saturated link (2.0) · Thermal throttling (3.0) · Overcommitted virtual machine (4.0) Is it slow on everything, or only on things that touch the network? Does it start fast and degrade as it warms? Does drive activity stay pinned with nothing running?
“It keeps shutting off.” Overheating (3.0) · Failing power supply (3.0) · Swollen battery (1.0) Does it happen under load or at idle? Is it a sudden cut or an orderly shutdown? On a portable, does it run indefinitely on external power?
“I can’t get to the internet.” No address (2.0) · Name resolution failure (2.0) · Wireless signal (2.0) · Physical link (3.0) Can the host reach a numeric address but not a name? Does it have a valid address at all? Does a wired connection behave differently from wireless?
“The screen looks wrong.” Display panel or backlight (3.0) · Cable or connector (3.0) · Resolution setting (3.0) · Digitizer (1.0) Is the image absent, distorted, or just badly sized? Does an external display behave the same way? Does touch input land where the finger actually is?
“Printing is messed up.” Consumable or imaging component (3.0) · Feed mechanism (3.0) · Queue or driver (2.0) · Connectivity (2.0) Does the defect repeat at a fixed interval down the page? Do jobs from every computer fail, or only one? Does a test page printed from the device itself look correct?

The Question That Does the Most Work

If you only get to ask one diagnostic question, make it this one: does the problem follow the device, the user, or the location?

A fault that follows the laptop into another room is in the laptop. One that stays in the room while the laptop works fine elsewhere is in the infrastructure. One that follows the person to a different machine is in their account or their configuration. That single question routes you into the right domain faster than any tool you can run.

Hardware Troubleshooting, Consolidated

This is the Unit 1 and Unit 2 material, returned to as diagnostic patterns rather than component descriptions.

SymptomMost Likely CausesFirst Moves
No power at all — no fans, no lights Dead outlet or cable, failed power supply, front panel power switch, incorrect voltage selector Confirm the outlet with a known-good device. Confirm the supply with a tester before condemning the board.
Power, but no display and no POST Unseated or failed memory, unseated graphics card, failed processor, failed board Reseat memory one module at a time. Try onboard video if present. Listen for POST codes or watch for board diagnostic indicators.
Random restarts or shutdowns under load Thermal event, inadequate or failing power supply, unstable memory Check fan operation and heatsink seating. Verify thermal paste and airflow. Confirm supply wattage against installed components.
Grinding or clicking, then missing files Mechanical drive failure in progress Stop using the drive immediately and image it if the data matters. Continued operation destroys recoverable data.
Array reports degraded but system still runs One member drive has failed in a redundant array Identify and replace the failed member, then allow the rebuild to complete before making further changes. A second failure during rebuild is the common way arrays are lost.
System will not boot from the expected drive Boot order changed, loose data or power cable, failed drive, firmware setting Check whether firmware sees the drive at all. If it does not, the problem is physical or firmware, not operating system.
POST

Simple terms: A self-check the system firmware runs before any operating system loads, verifying that the essential hardware needed to boot is present and responding. Real-world example: A machine that gives beep codes or diagnostic lights and never reaches a logo screen has failed this check — which tells you the fault is in core hardware, and saves you from wasting an hour on operating system repair.

Thermal Throttling

Simple terms: A processor deliberately slowing itself down to shed heat when cooling cannot keep up, trading performance for survival. Real-world example: A laptop that benchmarks normally for ninety seconds and then runs at half speed is not failing — it is protecting itself, and the actual fix is dust in the heatsink or dried thermal paste, not a replacement processor.

Display, Printer, and Mobile Issues, Consolidated

SymptomMost Likely CausesFirst Moves
Very dim image, visible under bright light Backlight or inverter failure Shine a light at the panel. If the image is there but unlit, the panel is fine and the backlight path is not.
Image is fuzzy or stretched Running outside the display’s native resolution, or incorrect aspect ratio Set the display to its native resolution before investigating anything else.
Flickering, color shifts, or missing color channel Loose or damaged video cable, failing connector, cable run too long Swap the cable first. It is the cheapest variable and the most common cause.
Defect repeats at a fixed interval down every page Damaged imaging drum or roller — the interval equals the circumference of the failed part Measure the spacing and match it to a component. That measurement identifies the part without disassembly.
Toner rubs off the finished page Fuser not reaching temperature, or wrong media type selected Verify the media setting matches what is loaded, then evaluate the fuser.
Jobs from one computer fail, others print fine Driver, queue, or permission issue on that host Print a test page from the device itself. If it prints, the printer is not the problem.
Touch input lands away from where the finger is Digitizer fault or calibration loss, screen protector interference Confirm the display renders correctly. A perfect image with wrong touch position isolates the digitizer from the panel.
Battery drains quickly or the case bulges Aged cell, runaway background process, swollen battery A bulging battery is removed from service immediately. Never attempt to keep using or puncture it.
Swollen Batteries

A lithium-ion battery that has visibly expanded, is separating a case seam, or is lifting a trackpad is a safety hazard, not an inconvenience. Power the device down, stop charging it, do not press on or puncture the cell, and follow your organization’s disposal procedure. This appears on the exam and it matters far more in the shop.

Network Troubleshooting, Consolidated

This is Unit 3 and Unit 4 material, worked from the symptom backward. The most useful habit here is to move deliberately up from the physical layer rather than starting with the most complicated explanation.

SymptomWhat It Points AtFirst Moves
No link light on the adapter or switch port Physical layer — cable, port, or termination Swap to a known-good cable and a known-good port. Test the run if the problem follows the jack.
Address in the 169.254 range The host never received an address from a server Confirm physical connectivity first, then check whether the address server is reachable and has available leases.
Numeric addresses work, names do not Name resolution Verify the configured resolver and test resolution directly against it.
Local resources reachable, remote ones are not Default gateway or routing Confirm the gateway address is correct and reachable, then trace the path outward to find where it stops.
Intermittent wireless, worse at distance Signal strength, interference, or channel overlap Check signal at the failing location, then look for competing networks and non-network interference sources on the same band.
One department affected, others fine Segment-level device or configuration Find what that group shares and nobody else does — a switch, an access point, a segment, a set of policies.
Scope of Impact

Simple terms: How many users or systems a problem touches, used as a diagnostic shortcut to locate where the fault must be. Real-world example: One user offline points at that workstation; a whole floor offline points at the switch or uplink serving that floor; the entire site offline points at the edge device or the provider — and knowing this lets you skip straight to the right equipment instead of starting at somebody’s desk.

Evidence and Elimination

Performance-based questions rarely ask you to recall a fact. They give you a scenario where two explanations both fit, and they score whether you chose the one the evidence supports. That is the skill this section is built around.

Worked Example: Two Diagnoses, One Symptom

A user reports that file transfers to the department server have become extremely slow this week. Nothing was changed on their workstation. Two theories fit:

TheoryWhat Would Be True If This Were It
A. The workstation’s drive is failing Local file operations would also be slow. Drive activity would stay high with nothing running. Other users would be unaffected.
B. The network path to the server is congested or degraded Local operations would be normal. Other users on the same segment would report the same problem. The slowdown would track time of day or load.

You do not need both answers. You need one test that produces different results depending on which theory is true — here, copying a large file from one local folder to another. Fast local copy eliminates Theory A outright. That is the whole method: find the test that splits the theories, then run that test.

Explaining It to the Customer

Every diagnosis you reach has to survive one more step: telling the person what happened in words they can use. Skip the component names and give them the shape of it. “The part that stores your files is wearing out. I moved your data to a healthy drive before it got worse, and everything is back where you left it.” That is a complete, honest, useful explanation with no jargon in it, and delivering it well is as much a part of this job as the repair was.
