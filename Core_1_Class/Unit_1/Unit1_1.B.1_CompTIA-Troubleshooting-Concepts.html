1.B.1 — CompTIA Troubleshooting Concepts

Learning Objectives

After completing this guide, you will be able to:

  1. Recite and apply the six steps of the CompTIA troubleshooting methodology in sequence
  2. Identify when to repeat steps 2–3 when an initial theory proves incorrect
  3. Explain why questioning users about recent changes is central to Step 1
  4. Produce thorough documentation of troubleshooting findings and solutions (Step 6)

The CompTIA Six-Step Troubleshooting Methodology

The CompTIA troubleshooting methodology is a structured, repeatable process that ensures technicians approach problems systematically rather than guessing at solutions. This is not just exam content — it's a professional framework you will use for the rest of your career.

Why Methodology Matters

Random troubleshooting wastes time, can create new problems, and frustrates users. A systematic approach ensures you identify the root cause rather than just a symptom, don't skip obvious checks, can explain your process to a supervisor or a customer, and leave documentation for whoever handles the next ticket. The methodology also tells you when a problem has genuinely exceeded your ability and needs to be escalated.

Critical Iteration Point

If your theory proves incorrect during Step 3 (Test the Theory), you return to Step 2 and establish a new theory. This loop continues until you find the actual cause. Do not skip ahead to implementing a fix without confirming your theory first.

Detailed Step Breakdown

Step 1
Identify the Problem

This is the foundation of all troubleshooting. Rushing past this step is the single biggest cause of wasted time and wrong fixes.

Always Ask About Recent Changes

The single most useful question you can ask is "What changed recently?" Software installs or updates, new hardware, moved equipment, new users, configuration changes, and power events all count. A large share of the problems you'll see trace back to something that changed shortly before the symptom appeared.

Key Actions:

  • Gather information from the user — what happened, when, and any error messages
  • Ask about recent changes or unusual events
  • Reproduce the problem yourself if possible
  • Identify the actual symptoms, not just the user's interpretation of them
  • Check logs, Event Viewer, and any error messages
  • Consider environmental factors — power, temperature, network
  • Determine whether other users or systems are affected too
Step 2
Establish a Theory of Probable Cause

Based on what you gathered in Step 1, develop an educated theory about what's actually causing the problem.

Key Actions:

  • Consider the most likely cause first — question the obvious
  • Use your own knowledge and experience to narrow the possibilities
  • Rank multiple candidate theories by probability rather than picking one blindly
  • Check knowledge bases or documentation for similar reported issues
  • Recognize early if this is already beyond your skill level and needs escalation
Question the Obvious

Check the simple things first: Is it plugged in? Is the switch on? Is the cable actually seated? Is the right input selected? These checks solve more problems than any complex theory does.

Step 3
Test the Theory to Determine Cause

Confirm your theory before making any permanent change. This step is what decides whether you move forward or loop back.

Key Actions:

  • Run a test that will actually confirm or rule out your theory
  • Use diagnostic tools appropriate to the type of problem
  • Make a temporary change to test with, rather than a permanent fix
  • If confirmed: move to Step 4
  • If not confirmed: return to Step 2 with a new theory
  • If nothing can be confirmed, escalate to a more senior technician
Loop point: return to Step 2 if the theory fails
Step 4
Establish a Plan of Action and Implement the Solution

With the cause confirmed, plan the fix carefully, then carry it out. Planning and doing are one step because a fix implemented without a plan is how new problems get created.

Key Actions — Planning:

  • Work out the specific steps needed to fix the problem
  • Consider what else the fix might affect
  • Back up data first if there's any risk to it
  • Schedule downtime if the fix requires it, and get approval if others are affected
  • Have a rollback plan ready in case the fix doesn't work

Key Actions — Implementing:

  • Follow the plan, making one change at a time where possible
  • Document each action as you take it, not from memory afterward
  • Keep the user informed of progress and expected downtime
  • Escalate if the fix turns out to require access, skill, or authority you don't have
Step 5
Verify Full System Functionality

Don't assume the fix worked. Verify it, and confirm you haven't broken something else in the process.

Key Actions:

  • Test the specific function that was originally failing
  • Have the user confirm it too — their workflow may exercise the system differently than yours does
  • Check related functions to make sure nothing else broke
  • Put preventive measures in place if this is likely to recur
  • Keep an eye out for recurrence over the following days
Step 6
Document Findings, Actions, and Outcomes

Documentation is the step most often skipped, and the one that matters most for whoever handles the next related ticket — possibly you.

Never Skip Documentation

Document even when you're busy. It reveals patterns across multiple tickets, protects you if the issue resurfaces, and becomes training material for whoever comes after you.

What to Document:

  • The original problem description and symptoms
  • What you asked and what the user said, especially about recent changes
  • Every theory tested, including the ones that didn't pan out
  • The actual root cause once identified
  • The solution you implemented
  • How you verified it
  • Time spent and any parts or software used
  • Any recommendation to prevent the same issue recurring

Detailed Example: When the First Theory Fails

Here's a realistic scenario where the first theory doesn't pan out, requiring a loop back through the process.

Scenario: Desktop Won't Turn On

User
Sarah Chen, Accounting
Equipment
Dell OptiPlex 7090
Reported Issue
"My computer won't turn on"
Ticket #
INC-0417
FIRST PASS THROUGH THE METHODOLOGY

Step 1: Identify the Problem

User interview:

  • "When I press the power button, nothing happens at all."
  • "I first noticed it this morning when I came in."
  • "It was working fine when I left yesterday at 5 PM."

Questions about recent changes:

  • Q: "Did you install any software or updates recently?" A: "No, not that I know of."
  • Q: "Was any equipment moved or cables touched?" A: "No, everything is the same."
  • Q: "Any power outages or storms overnight?" A: "Actually, yes — there was a brief power flicker around 2 AM. Security mentioned it."
  • Q: "Are any other devices in your area not working?" A: "My desk lamp is on, so the outlet works."

Observations:

  • No LEDs lit on the computer, not even standby
  • No fan noise when the power button is pressed
  • Monitor works — shows "No Signal" with a known-good cable
  • Desk lamp confirms the wall outlet has power

Step 2: Establish a Theory (First Attempt)

Theory #1: The overnight power flicker damaged the power supply unit.

Reasoning: power events commonly damage PSUs, and "no power at all" is consistent with PSU failure.

Step 3: Test the Theory (First Attempt)

Test performed: Swapped in a known-good PSU from spare inventory.

Result: Still no signs of life — no LEDs, no fans.

Theory Not Confirmed

The PSU swap didn't solve it. Back to Step 2 with a new theory.

SECOND PASS THROUGH STEPS 2–3

Step 2: Establish a Theory (Second Attempt)

Theory #2: The power event damaged the motherboard, preventing POST even with a known-good PSU.

Reasoning: if a good PSU still produces no power indication, the motherboard's power circuitry is the next likely suspect.

Step 3: Test the Theory (Second Attempt)

Tests performed:

  • Inspected the board for visible damage — no bulging capacitors or burn marks found
  • Tested the PSU connection with a PSU tester — tests good
  • Attempted to power on with minimum components (CPU, one RAM stick, no drives) — still nothing
  • Checked the power button's connection to the motherboard — secure
  • Tried shorting the power pins directly to bypass the switch — still nothing

Result: The motherboard appears to have failed at the power circuit. Even a direct short produces no response.

Theory Confirmed

The motherboard has failed. Proceed to Step 4.

COMPLETING THE METHODOLOGY

Step 4: Plan and Implement

Plan:

  1. Check warranty status — Dell ProSupport is active
  2. Contact Dell to arrange a motherboard replacement
  3. Set Sarah up with a loaner workstation in the meantime
  4. Move her drive to the loaner or connect it via a USB adapter so she can keep working
  5. Schedule the Dell technician visit
  6. After the repair, restore her original drive and confirm every application still works

Implementation:

  • Contacted Dell — technician scheduled for the next day
  • Retrieved a loaner workstation from IT storage
  • Connected Sarah's drive to the loaner via USB adapter and confirmed her data was accessible
  • Configured the loaner with her network credentials
  • Dell technician replaced the motherboard the following day

Step 5: Verify Full System Functionality

  • Computer powers on normally
  • Windows boots without errors
  • All applications launch correctly
  • Sarah confirmed access to her accounting software and reports
  • Network connectivity and printers working
  • Connected the machine to a UPS as a preventive measure going forward

Step 6: Document Findings, Actions, and Outcomes

Ticket #INC-0417 — Resolution Notes

Problem: Desktop would not power on — no LEDs, no fans, no POST.

Root Cause: Motherboard power circuit failure following an overnight power flicker.

Troubleshooting Steps:
1. First theory: PSU failure — ruled out via PSU swap
2. Second theory: motherboard failure — confirmed via minimum-boot test and a direct power-pin short

Resolution: Dell motherboard replacement under warranty. User provided a loaner workstation during the repair.

Preventive Action: Workstation connected to a UPS to guard against future power events.

Time Spent: 1.5 hours troubleshooting, plus a 1-hour vendor visit.
Recommendation: Consider UPS deployment across accounting workstations given the sensitivity of the data involved.
Key Takeaways from This Example

Step 1 matters most: asking about recent changes surfaced the overnight power event — the actual root cause. Without that question, this would have taken much longer to diagnose.

Iteration is normal, not failure: the first theory (PSU failure) was reasonable and still wrong. Rather than guessing further, the technician returned to Step 2 with what the failed test had actually revealed.

Step 6 did real work: the documentation captured not just what fixed it, but the theory that didn't, plus a preventive recommendation that could help the whole department, not just this one ticket.
