4.11 — Unit 4 Tickets
Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three help-desk tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you? You will come back to it.
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them from the user's computer.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution, below the solution itself. Answer them in your notes. They are about your thinking, not the content, and nobody else will see them.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • If you miss one, go back to the exhibit and the call and work out why. That search is the lesson.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note. The Look Back prompts are inside it.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of the output from the computer being troubleshot — what you would see on that machine's screen if you opened the tool or settings window, or ran the command shown. Treat them as evidence you collected yourself during the call.

They are simplified to show what matters, the same way a performance-based question on the 220-1202 exam presents a tool or a command's output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA's troubleshooting method it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and implement the solution
  5. Verify full system functionality and, if applicable, implement preventive measures
  6. Document findings, actions, and outcomes

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Ticket 1 of 3 The Invoice Nobody Sent Tuesday · Objectives 2.4 and 2.5 — Malware and social engineering · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-4-1020
PriorityCritical — attempted fraud plus a possibly infected finance workstation
CategorySecurity incident
CallerGloria Hutchins, Controller, Summit Ridge Builders
DeviceAsset SRB-FIN-02 · Finance desktop · Windows 11 Pro
The Call

"I almost wired forty-eight thousand dollars this morning. An email came from our CEO, Mark Delaney, saying a vendor changed banks and the payment had to go out today, and to keep it quiet. It looked exactly like him. I only stopped because the vendor happened to call about something else. And separately — my computer's been really slow since I opened an attachment from 'the bank' last week."

The company's real email domain is summitridgebuilders.com. No money was sent.

Stop Here

Before the exhibits, write down whether you think the email and the slow computer are connected, and how sure you are.

Exhibit A — Header of the payment email

From:      "Mark Delaney" <mdelaney@summitridge-builders.co>
Reply-To:  mdelaney.office@mailbox-secure.example
To:        ghutchins@summitridgebuilders.com
Subject:   Urgent - vendor banking update - keep confidential

Exhibit B — Task Manager on SRB-FIN-02, Processes sorted by CPU

NameCPUMemoryNetwork
svchost32.exe92%610 MB0.4 Mbps
Microsoft Edge (6)3%910 MB0.1 Mbps
Accounting Suite1%420 MB0 Mbps

No files are renamed or locked, no pop-ups appear, and the fan runs constantly. The genuine Windows process is named svchost.exe, not svchost32.exe.

Exhibit C — The company's current accounts payable procedure

Vendor banking changes: update the vendor record in the accounting
system when an authorized manager requests it by email.

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — this is a security incident, so record what you observed and when, without guessing at who did it. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Reported: controller nearly sent a $48,000 wire after an email impersonating the CEO; separately, her workstation has been slow since she opened an attachment last week. No money sent.

Found: the email came from a look-alike domain (summitridge-builders.co) with replies going to an outside mailbox — business email compromise. On the workstation, a process named svchost32.exe imitating a Windows process uses 92% of the CPU, with no files encrypted and no pop-ups — consistent with a cryptominer.

Action: disconnected SRB-FIN-02 from the network and reported both findings to management and the security contact under the incident procedure. Preserved the email and its full headers. Machine held for malware removal; no changes made to it that would destroy evidence.

Preventive: recommended that banking changes require phone confirmation using a number already on file, and that the look-alike domain be blocked.

Look Back

  1. Gloria stopped the wire because of a lucky phone call. What would have stopped it by design rather than by luck?
  2. You were asked whether the email and the slow computer were connected. The honest answer today is "not proven." How do you write that in a ticket without guessing?
  3. She is shaken and embarrassed. What do you say first to someone who almost lost $48,000?
  4. Who needed to hear about this, and in what order? Did you think of anyone beyond your own manager?
Ticket 2 of 3 The Clinic Walk-Through Wednesday · Objectives 2.1 and 2.7 — Security measures and workstation hardening · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-4-1027
PriorityMedium — findings before an insurance audit
CategorySecurity review
CallerDr. Naomi Ford, Practice Owner, Ford Pediatrics
DeviceFront desk and exam-room workstations · Windows 11 Pro
The Call

"Our insurance auditor comes next month and I want to fix the obvious things first. Can you walk through the office and tell me what's wrong? Please be honest — I'd much rather hear it from you than from them."

You walk through the clinic as an observer. You note what you see; you do not test doors or try passwords.

Stop Here

Before the exhibits, write down the three things you expect to find in a small medical office.

Exhibit A — Your walk-through notes

Main entranceBadge reader on the door. Staff hold it open for whoever is behind them, including people they do not know.
ReceptionMonitor faces the waiting room.
Exam room 2PC unlocked and signed in, unattended for 15 minutes or more. A password on a sticky note under the keyboard.
Server closetDoor propped open with a box.

Exhibit B — Output of net user on the exam-room PC

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Exhibit C — Local Security Policy > Account Lockout Policy

Account lockout durationNot applicable
Reset account lockout counter afterNot applicable

Close the Ticket

Write your findings first, as a short list you would hand to Dr. Ford — most serious first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Findings, most serious first: (1) Shared "frontdesk" account — nobody's actions can be traced to a person. (2) No account lockout — unlimited password guessing. (3) Guest account active. (4) Unattended unlocked PC and a password on a sticky note. (5) Tailgating at the badge door. (6) Server closet left open. (7) Reception monitor visible from the waiting room.

Fixed today, with her approval: disabled Guest; set the lockout threshold to 5 attempts; set screen lock after 5 minutes; removed the sticky note and had the password changed; closed and locked the server closet.

Recommended: individual accounts for every staff member; a privacy screen at reception; a no-holding-the-door rule now, and an access control vestibule if budget allows.

Documented: a findings memo for the audit file, noting each item, what was done, and what remains.

Look Back

  1. Dr. Ford asked you to be honest. How do you deliver seven findings to someone without overwhelming or embarrassing her?
  2. Which finding was cheapest to fix and most valuable? Why does that kind of fix matter so much in a small office?
  3. The shared "frontdesk" account weakens almost every other control. Explain why in one sentence.
  4. You observed the door but did not test it. Why is that boundary important, even when you are trying to help?
Ticket 3 of 3 Clearing Out the Storage Room Thursday · Objectives 2.9, 4.5, and 4.6 — Disposal, environment, and policy · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-4-1103
PriorityHigh — regulated data, a safety hazard, and a possible incident
CategoryAsset disposal · Safety · Incident
CallerVictor Huang, Office Manager, Lakeside Physical Therapy
DeviceRetired equipment: 8 laptops, 1 file server, 1 UPS
The Call

"We're cleaning out the storage room. There are eight old laptops, an old server, and a UPS in the corner that looks kind of puffy. A recycling company offered to haul all of it away for free tomorrow — can we just let them? Oh, and while I was boxing things up, one laptop was still signed in, and there were some files on it I really don't think should be on a work computer. I copied them to a flash drive so I could show you."

The old file server held patient records. The laptops belonged to former staff.

Stop Here

Three separate problems in one call. Before the exhibits, write down which one you would deal with first, and why.

Exhibit A — Equipment inventory

Exhibit B — The recycling company's offer

Exhibit C — What Victor did

StepAction
1Found laptop LPT-LT-06 still signed in to a former employee's account
2Opened a folder and saw files he believes may be illegal
3Copied the files to a personal USB flash drive "to show you"
4Closed the laptop lid

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — and note anything about the flash drive that matters for what happens next. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

First, the incident: told Victor to stop and not open, copy, or delete anything further. Secured laptop LPT-LT-06 and Victor's flash drive, labeled them, and began a chain-of-custody record noting Victor's copy. Escalated to management under the incident response procedure, which decides whether law enforcement is notified. The laptop was removed from the disposal group.

Disposal held: declined the free pickup. The server's drives, which held patient records, go to a vendor that provides a certificate of destruction. The laptop SSDs are sanitized with a secure erase, or destroyed, with documentation; degaussing does not work on solid-state drives.

Safety: moved the swollen UPS out of service to a ventilated area away from anything flammable, and arranged disposal according to the battery's safety data sheet and local regulations.

Look Back

  1. "Free pickup tomorrow" sounded like a gift. What made it tempting, and what would it have cost?
  2. Victor copied the files "to show you." How do you explain that this made things harder, without making him defensive?
  3. Which of the three items carried the most risk — data, safety, or legal? Did you handle it first?
  4. Finding something like this is uncomfortable. What will you do in the moment to stay calm and follow the procedure?
What These Tickets Give You
  • Recognizing business email compromise and a cryptominer from the evidence they leave behind
  • Delivering honest security findings to an owner, most serious first, without testing anything you were not authorized to test
  • Handling regulated data, a safety hazard, and a possible incident in the same call, in the right order
  • Progress notes another technician could pick up without asking you a single question
