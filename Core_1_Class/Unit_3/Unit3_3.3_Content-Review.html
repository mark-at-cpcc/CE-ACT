Technical Overview

This section walks through the core concepts for Unit 3 before you hit the full chapter reading in 3.4: network cables and connectors, common network hardware, IPv4/IPv6 addressing fundamentals, ports and protocols, internet connection and network types, and the tools that go with all of it.

In Plain Terms

Think of this section as the trailer, not the movie. It gives you the shape of the week so the full reading in 3.4 has something to attach to, instead of hitting you cold.

Network Cables & Connectors

Every network relies on some form of network media — the physical pathway that carries data from one device to another. There are two types of network media: wired and wireless. Both are physical media. Wired media carries signals as electrical pulses over copper or as light over fiber; wireless media carries signals as radio waves through the air. Wireless is still a physical medium — the radio waves are real, physical signals traveling through space — it simply doesn't use a cable you can hold in your hand.

This unit focuses entirely on wired connections: the cables, connectors, and wiring standards that make up a physical, cabled network. Wireless media — Wi-Fi standards, access point placement, and radio frequency behavior — is covered in a later unit. For now, every cable and connector below is part of the wired world.

UTP vs. STP

Unshielded twisted pair (UTP) is standard office/home cabling. Shielded twisted pair (STP) adds a foil or braided shield around the conductors to resist electrical interference — common near heavy machinery, elevator shafts, or long outdoor/direct-burial runs.

Plenum-Rated Cable

Cable jacketed with a fire-retardant, low-smoke material required by building code any time cable runs through a plenum space — the area above a drop ceiling or below a raised floor used for HVAC airflow.

Wire Pairs Inside the Cable

A standard twisted pair network cable contains four pairs of wires — eight individual conductors in total. Each pair is twisted together along the length of the cable, and the amount of twist is engineered specifically to cancel out electromagnetic interference between pairs. This is why it's called "twisted pair": the twisting isn't decorative, it's what keeps the signal clean. All four pairs (eight conductors) are what get terminated into an RJ45 connector following the T568A or T568B standard, and modern Gigabit Ethernet uses all four pairs to move data.

By contrast, common telephone cable typically contains only two pairs of wires — four conductors — and often uses just one pair for a single phone line. This is one reason a telephone connector (RJ11) is physically narrower than a network connector (RJ45): it simply has fewer conductors to accommodate. You cannot reliably run modern Ethernet over standard telephone cable, because the missing pairs and the lower construction quality can't carry the signal.

Don't Confuse the Two

An RJ11 telephone jack and an RJ45 network jack look similar at a glance, and an RJ11 plug will even fit loosely into an RJ45 port. They are not interchangeable — the conductor count and wiring are different, and mixing them will not produce a working network connection.

Coaxial Cable Construction and Limitations

Coaxial cable is built very differently from twisted pair. Instead of multiple twisted pairs, it uses a single central copper conductor that carries the signal, surrounded in layers: a plastic insulating layer (the dielectric) around the center conductor, then a braided metal shield (and often a foil layer) that blocks outside interference, and finally an outer protective jacket. The name "coaxial" comes from the fact that the center conductor and the shield share the same central axis.

That heavy shielding gives coaxial cable strong resistance to electromagnetic interference, which is why it was long used for cable television and cable internet service, terminated with an F-connector. Its main limitations compared to twisted pair and fiber are that it is thicker and less flexible, harder to route and terminate in tight spaces, and it carries a single signal path rather than the multiple pairs that let twisted pair handle full-duplex Gigabit traffic. In modern networking it survives mainly as the cable that delivers internet from the provider to a cable modem, rather than as wiring inside the local network itself.

T568A and T568B Wiring Standards

Both standards define the same eight-conductor color order, just with the green and orange pairs swapped:

  • T568A: White/Green, Green, White/Orange, Blue, White/Blue, Orange, White/Brown, Brown
  • T568B: White/Orange, Orange, White/Green, Blue, White/Blue, Green, White/Brown, Brown

A cable wired to the same standard on both ends is a straight-through cable, used for connecting dissimilar devices (computer to switch). A cable wired to T568A on one end and T568B on the other is a crossover cable, historically used to connect similar devices directly (switch to switch). Most modern NICs auto-detect and correct for this, a technology called Auto-MDIX — covered next.

Auto-MDIX

Auto-MDIX (Automatic Medium-Dependent Interface Crossover) is a technology built into modern network ports that automatically detects how a cable is wired and adjusts the port's transmit and receive pairs to match. In practical terms, it means the port figures out whether it needs a straight-through or a crossover connection and corrects for it on its own.

Because nearly all current NICs, switches, and routers include Auto-MDIX, the distinction between straight-through and crossover cables is effectively obsolete in the real world — you can generally plug any properly terminated twisted pair cable between two devices and it will work. That said, straight-through and crossover cables are still fully functional, and the wiring standards behind them remain fair game on the exam. Older equipment without Auto-MDIX still depends on the correct cable type, so knowing the difference matters even though the technology has made it a non-issue on modern hardware.

One Box, Many Devices

A typical home "router" is usually a router, switch, access point, modem, and basic firewall combined into a single unit. On the exam and in the field, be ready to identify each function separately even when they live in one physical box.

Logical and Physical Addressing

Every device on a network carries two different kinds of address at the same time, and they do two different jobs. Confusing the two is one of the most common early mistakes in networking, so it's worth getting straight before we go further into IP.

Physical Address (MAC Address)

A physical address is permanently tied to a piece of hardware — specifically the network interface that connects a device to the network. These are called MAC addresses. Every physical address is globally unique, burned into the hardware at the factory, and it identifies that one specific interface and no other. Physical addresses are not routed onto the Internet; they stay on the local network, which keeps a device's permanent hardware identity from being exposed publicly.

Logical Address (IP Address)

A logical address is assigned by software rather than built into hardware, and it can be reused. These are IP addresses, assigned to a known host or NIC connection either manually (static) or automatically by DHCP (dynamic). Because they are assigned rather than permanent, logical addresses can change — a laptop gets one address on the office network and a different one at home — and the same private address can be reused on thousands of separate networks.

How the Two Work Together

Think of the logical address as a street address and the physical address as the specific mailbox that address belongs to. Follow a single message from the moment you hit send:

  • Leaving your device. Your computer knows the destination's IP address, but it can't reach the Internet on its own. It looks at the destination IP, sees it isn't on the local network, and addresses the frame to the MAC address of the router — the local doorway out. The destination IP stays untouched inside the packet the whole time.
  • Crossing your local network. The switch reads only the MAC address and forwards the frame to the correct port until it reaches the router. Switches never look at IP addresses.
  • Going out to the Internet. The router strips off the local MAC addressing, applies its own public IP, and sends the packet on using the destination IP address. This is the security point from earlier: your device's MAC address never leaves the local network.
  • Router to router. Across the Internet, each router reads the destination IP to decide the next hop, rewriting the MAC addressing at every step for the next link in the chain.
  • Arriving at the destination network. The final router uses the IP address to know the packet has arrived, then uses the MAC address of the destination device to hand it to the exact machine.

The pattern to remember: the IP address stays constant end to end and gets the message to the right network, while the MAC address changes at every hop and moves it across each individual network. Routers work on IP; switches work on MAC.

MAC Address Fundamentals

MAC Address

A Media Access Control address is a 48-bit address assigned to a network interface, written as twelve hexadecimal digits — six pairs separated by colons or hyphens, such as 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. Because it is hexadecimal, only the digits 0–9 and the letters A–F appear.

The Two Halves of a MAC Address

A MAC address splits evenly into two 24-bit halves, and knowing which half is which is commonly tested:

  • First half (first 6 hex digits) — OUI: the Organizationally Unique Identifier, assigned to the manufacturer. Every interface built by that manufacturer starts with the same OUI, which is why you can often identify a device's maker from its MAC address alone.
  • Second half (last 6 hex digits): the unique serial value the manufacturer assigns to that specific interface.

Together, the two halves guarantee that no two interfaces anywhere share the same address.

Finding a MAC Address

On Windows, ipconfig /all lists it as the Physical Address for each adapter. On Linux or macOS, ifconfig or ip a shows it. A device with both wired and wireless interfaces has a separate MAC address for each one — the address belongs to the interface, not to the computer as a whole.

MAC Spoofing

Although a MAC address is burned into the hardware, software can override the value a device reports. This is called MAC spoofing. It has legitimate uses, such as replacing a failed device without reconfiguring the network, but it is also used to bypass MAC filtering on a wireless network — which is why MAC filtering alone is not considered a strong security control.

IP Addressing Fundamentals

IPv4

A 32-bit address written as four decimal numbers (octets) separated by periods, e.g. 192.168.1.10. Each octet is made up of 8 bits, and 8 bits together form one byte — so every octet is exactly one byte, and the four octets add up to the full 32 bits of the address. The available address space is exhausted at the public internet level, which is part of why IPv6 exists.

Omitting Zeroes in an IPv6 Address

IPv6 addresses are long, so the standard allows two shortcuts to make them easier to write. Take the full address 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0370:7334 as an example.

Rule 1 — drop leading zeroes. Within any single hextet, leading zeroes can be removed. So 0db8 becomes db8, and 0370 becomes 370. A hextet of 0000 can be shortened all the way down to a single 0.

Rule 2 — collapse one run of all-zero hextets with a double colon. A single continuous run of hextets that are all zeroes can be replaced entirely by a double colon (::). Applying both rules, the example above becomes 2001:db8::370:7334.

The catch: the double colon can appear only once in an address. If two separate runs of zeroes existed, using :: for both would make it impossible to know how many zero hextets belong to each gap, so only the longer run gets collapsed and the other is written out. This one-time limit is the rule most often tested and most often missed.

CIDR Notation

CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing) notation is a shorthand way to write a subnet mask by adding a slash and a number to the end of an IP address — for example, 192.168.1.1/24. The number after the slash tells you how many bits, counting from the left, make up the network portion of the address.

Since an IPv4 address is 32 bits total, a /24 means the first 24 bits identify the network and the remaining 8 bits identify the host — which is the same thing as the subnet mask 255.255.255.0. Likewise /16 equals 255.255.0.0, and /8 equals 255.0.0.0. CIDR simply lets you express that same mask with two or three characters instead of writing out all four octets, which is why you'll see it everywhere in modern networking.

The Five Classes of IPv4 Addresses

Before CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing) became the modern standard, every IPv4 address belonged to one of five classes, determined by the value of the first octet. You'll still see class boundaries referenced constantly — especially because the private address ranges live inside them — so it's worth knowing all five, even though only Classes A, B, and C are the ones that actually matter day to day. Class D is reserved for multicast traffic and Class E is reserved for experimental use; neither is assigned to devices on a network.

Common Exam Trap

127 falls inside the Class A numeric range, but the entire 127.0.0.0/8 block is reserved for loopback addresses (127.0.0.1 is "localhost") and is never assigned to a real host. The usable Class A range for host addressing is effectively 1–126.

Each of the three usable classes sets aside its own private address block.

Private range addresses are only usable on a LAN, and are not routable onto the WAN. Only public addresses can be used on the WAN and are not usable on a LAN. Routers are the only network device that can hold either of these addresses. All other devices are one or the other.

Why This Still Matters

Real-world networks today use CIDR and variable-length subnet masks instead of rigid class boundaries, so you'll rarely hear someone say "that's a Class C network" in the field. But the class system is still the fastest way to recognize a private address on sight, and it explains why 172's private block is oddly narrow (172.16–172.31 only, not the full 172.0–172.255 Class B range) and why 192.168.0.0/16 is really 256 Class C–sized networks bundled together rather than one native Class B block.

Common Exam Trap

The 172 private range only covers 172.16.0.0 through 172.31.255.255. An address like 172.32.x.x or 172.10.x.x looks private at a glance but is actually a public address — check the second octet carefully.

APIPA (Automatic Private IP Addressing)

When a device is configured for DHCP but cannot reach a DHCP server, Windows assigns itself an address in the 169.254.0.0/16 range so it can still communicate on the local segment. Seeing a 169.254.x.x address on a device is a strong sign that DHCP is unreachable.

Static vs. Dynamic Addressing

Static addressing means a technician manually assigns and fixes an IP address on a device — common for servers, printers, and network hardware that other devices need to find at a predictable address. Dynamic addressing means a DHCP server assigns the address automatically, typically for lease renewal after a set period — the default for most client devices.

DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol)

DHCP is the service that makes dynamic addressing work. When a device joins the network, DHCP automatically hands it an IP address from a defined pool of available private addresses — for example, a range like 192.168.1.100 through 192.168.1.200 that the administrator sets aside for this purpose. The device borrows an address from that pool for a period of time (a lease) and returns it when it's no longer needed, so the same addresses can be reused by other devices. This is what lets a new laptop or phone connect and get on the network without anyone manually typing in an address.

Ports & Protocols

TCP vs. UDP

TCP is connection-oriented and reliable — it establishes a connection first and confirms delivery, at the cost of some overhead. Because it verifies that data arrives and re-sends anything that gets lost, TCP provides error correction, which makes it the right choice for important network traffic where every piece must arrive intact — file transfers, web pages, and email. UDP is connectionless and fast — it sends data without confirming delivery, favoring speed over reliability. That trade-off suits non-essential digital media traffic where a dropped piece here or there doesn't ruin the experience, such as streaming movies and music, live video, and online video games. Losing a single frame of video is better than pausing everything to re-send it, so that kind of traffic uses UDP.
