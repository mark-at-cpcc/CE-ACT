Five of Seven Objectives Are Scenario Objectives

More of this unit is "Given a scenario" than any other unit in the course. That makes hands-on practice worth more here than anywhere else — the exam will describe a situation and ask what you would configure, and the only reliable preparation is having configured it.

Record What You Changed, Not Just What You Fixed

This unit's tickets involve changing settings, and a changed setting that nobody wrote down is the cause of next month's mystery. "Set the share permission to Change and the NTFS permission to Modify for the Accounting group" is a progress note. "Fixed permissions" is not. This is change management at the smallest possible scale, and it is the habit the rest of the discipline is built on.

Lab 5.6.1 — Permissions Puzzle

Wednesday · In class · 45 minutes · Objective 2.2

Work in pairs, on paper first and a lab PC second. You will be given a series of folders, each with a share permission and an NTFS permission, and a user arriving either over the network or at the keyboard.

  1. Predict on paper. For each case, write down the user's effective access before touching a computer. Commit to an answer — a guess you wrote down teaches more than one you kept vague.
  2. Watch for the local case. At least one case has the user signed in at the machine rather than across the network. Decide what share permissions do in that situation.
  3. Test it. Configure the case on a lab PC and confirm the access from a second machine. Record any answer you got wrong and why.
  4. Break inheritance. Create a subfolder, disable inheritance, and set different permissions. Then create a file in each folder and predict what the new file receives.
  5. State the rule in your own words — in one sentence, without using the word "restrictive." If you can do that, you own it.

The Case People Miss

Share permissions apply only to users arriving over the network. A user sitting at the machine is governed by NTFS alone. This is why a locked-down share can still be wide open to anyone who can sign in locally — and it is why physical security and logical security are not separate topics.

Lab 5.6.2 — Harden the Router

Thursday · In class · 45–60 minutes · Objective 2.10

Using the lab SOHO router, work through the six-step checklist from Section 5.3 on real hardware, in order.

  1. Find the defaults first. Before changing anything, look up this router's default administrator credentials online. Record how long it took you. That number is the lesson.
  2. Change the administrator password and record where that setting lives.
  3. Check the firmware version against the manufacturer's current release, and record whether the device is behind.
  4. Set the SSID to something that identifies neither the hardware nor the owner, and note what the default revealed.
  5. Set encryption to WPA3, or WPA2 with AES if any device requires it. Record which options the router offers, including any you would never choose.
  6. Disable UPnP and record where the setting was and what the router says about it.
  7. Restrict management access to the internal network, and confirm the administrative interface is not reachable from outside.
  8. Configure guest access and verify a guest client cannot reach an internal device.
Restore the Lab Router Before You Leave

Record every setting before you change it, and put the router back the way you found it at the end of the session, or the next group starts with credentials they do not have. This is also the rollback plan from objective 4.2, in miniature — written before the change, not after.

Lab 5.6.3 — BitLocker To Go

Thursday · In class · 45 minutes · Objectives 2.2, 2.7

On a Windows Pro lab PC, encrypt a USB drive and then find out what that actually means on a different machine.

  1. Start with an empty drive. Use a lab flash drive, not your own, and confirm it holds nothing you need — assume anything on it will be lost.
  2. Copy a test file to the drive and open it, so you know it works before encryption.
  3. Enable BitLocker To Go, choosing a password. Record where the recovery key is stored and what your options were.
  4. Move the drive to a second machine and try to open the file. Record exactly what happens and what the prompt says.
  5. Unlock it with the password, then record what a user would experience if they had forgotten it and had no recovery key.
  6. Decrypt the drive and return it before the end of class.
The Recovery Key Is the Whole Lesson

Encryption that works is indistinguishable from data loss when the credential is gone. Every real BitLocker deployment lives or dies on where the recovery keys are kept and who can reach them. A technician who enables encryption without answering that question first has not secured the data — they have scheduled its destruction.

Lab 5.6.4 — Take-Home, Part A: Secure Your Own Network

On your own · 45 minutes · Ungraded · Objective 2.10

Most people have never signed in to the router sitting in their home. This unit is when that changes.

Your Network Only, and Audit Before You Change
  • Do this only on a network you own or administer. A dormitory, apartment-complex, or employer network is not yours to configure.
  • If you cannot sign in to the router, record that as your finding and do the rest from what you can observe. Do not reset the device.
  • Record every setting before you change anything, and change only what you are confident about.
  • If a change would interrupt service for other people in the household, ask them first or leave it as a recommendation.
  1. Sign in, and record whether the administrator password is still the default.
  2. Firmware: record the installed version and whether a newer one is available.
  3. SSID: record it, and say what it reveals about the hardware or the household.
  4. Encryption: record the standard in use, and which stronger options the router supports.
  5. UPnP: record whether it is enabled.
  6. Guest network: record whether one exists and whether it is separated from internal devices.
  7. Management access: record whether the administrative interface is reachable from outside the network.
  8. Connected devices: list what is currently connected. Note anything you cannot identify.

Write it up as a short memo: what you found, which items you changed, and which you are recommending rather than doing. End with one line: which single finding would you fix first, and why that one?

Lab 5.6.5 — Take-Home, Part B: Lock Down Your Browser

On your own · 45 minutes · Ungraded · Objective 2.11

The browser is the program most exposed to hostile content and the one people configure least. Audit your own.

  1. Version and updates. Record your browser's version and when it last updated. Note whether updates apply automatically.
  2. Inventory your extensions. List every one, and for each record what it does, where it came from, and what permissions it holds. Flag any you cannot remember installing.
  3. Remove one. Choose an extension you cannot justify — by usefulness or by trust — and remove it. Record which and why. If every extension survives the test, say so and defend one of them.
  4. Check a certificate. Visit a site you trust, open the certificate details, and record who issued it and when it expires.
  5. Audit the settings from objective 2.11: pop-up blocker, saved passwords, sync, ad blocker, proxy, and secure DNS. Record the state of each and whether it is what you intended.
  6. Sync, deliberately. Record what is currently synchronized to your account, and decide whether you would want that on a work computer.
  7. Clear the cache and note anything that changed — sites you were signed out of, pages that loaded differently.

Write it up in a few sentences: what surprised you in your own browser, what you changed, and what you would tell a user who asked whether extensions are safe.

Two Parts, One Point

Part A secures the network a household uses. Part B secures the program a person actually spends their day inside. Together they cover the two places where real people are most often compromised — and neither one requires a lab, a license, or anything you do not already own.

What These Labs Give You

  • Effective access predicted on paper and confirmed on a real share, including the local-sign-in case
  • A SOHO router hardened end to end on real hardware, starting with how quickly its defaults can be found online
  • First-hand knowledge of what encrypted removable media does on a machine that does not have the key
  • An audit of your own home network, written as a memo with one priority named
  • An honest inventory of your own browser extensions, and one fewer than you started with
