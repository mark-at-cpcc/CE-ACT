Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three help-desk tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you? You will come back to it.
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them from the user's computer.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution, below the solution itself. Answer them in your notes. They are about your thinking, not the content, and nobody else will see them.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • If you miss one, go back to the exhibit and the call and work out why. That search is the lesson.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note. The Look Back prompts are inside it.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of the output from the computer being troubleshot — what you would see on that machine's screen if you opened the tool or settings window, or ran the command shown. Treat them as evidence you collected yourself during the call.

They are simplified to show what matters, the same way a performance-based question on the 220-1202 exam presents a tool or a command's output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA's troubleshooting method it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and implement the solution
  5. Verify full system functionality and, if applicable, implement preventive measures
  6. Document findings, actions, and outcomes

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Ticket Solution

Reported: new accounting hire can read but not save in the Accounting share, despite Full Control reportedly granted yesterday; also wants to take reports home on USB.

Found: yesterday's Full Control was a share permission. NTFS grants his group, Accounting-ReadOnly, only Read & execute. Over the network the more restrictive permission applies, so his effective access was Read.

Action: with the manager's confirmation of his role, moved him from Accounting-ReadOnly to Accounting-Staff (Modify); he signed out and in, and saved a test entry. Enabled BitLocker To Go on the company USB stick, with the recovery key stored in IT's escrow.

Note for the team: permission requests should be checked at both the share and NTFS levels; the share permission for Everyone is broader than needed and was flagged for review.

Look Back

  1. "IT already gave him Full Control" was partly true. How do you correct a colleague's change in a ticket without making it sound like a mistake on their part?
  2. You changed a group, not Ben's individual permissions. Why does that matter six months from now?
  3. Did you check both permission sets before you answered Question 1? What does that tell you about how you read exhibits?
  4. It was month-end and everyone wanted a fast fix. When do you push back on "just give him admin rights"?

"A patient told my receptionist that our Wi-Fi password is on a sticky note in the waiting room, and he's been using it for months. He also said our 'router login page' came up when he typed some address on his phone. Now I'm worried. What's open that shouldn't be?"

Staff computers and patients share the same wireless network. The front desk PC holds the scheduling system.

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — list what you changed today and what you scheduled. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Reported: wireless password visible to patients; a patient reached the router's login page.

Found: factory-default administrator password; remote management enabled from the internet; outdated firmware; WPA2 with TKIP; patients on the staff network; UPnP enabled and had forwarded Remote Desktop on the front desk PC to the internet.

Action, with the owner's approval and after hours: changed the administrator password; disabled remote management; updated firmware; disabled UPnP and removed both forwarding rules; set WPA2 with AES (WPA3 not supported by two staff devices); created a separate guest network for patients with its own password; changed the staff password and removed the sticky note.

Follow-up: checked the front desk PC's logs for unexpected Remote Desktop sign-ins and reported the result to the owner.

Look Back

  1. A patient found the problem first. How do you talk to Dr. Ruiz about that without making her feel careless?
  2. Which single finding was the most dangerous? Did you choose it first, or did you work through the router in screen order?
  3. Router changes knock everyone off Wi-Fi for a few minutes. When should they happen, and who needs to know beforehand?
  4. Why does a separate guest network matter more than a stronger password? Answer in one sentence.

Write your review of Tom's request first — approve, reject, or return it with conditions, and say why. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Decision: returned the request with conditions rather than approving it.

Reasons: no rollback plan; Friday 4:30 PM is a poor window for a change to a server that Monday's closings depend on, with no one available over the weekend; the backups have never been test-restored, so "we're covered" has not been proven; and the backups are stored in the same room as the server.

Conditions: test-restore a sample of files to an alternate location first; write a rollback plan, including the previous firmware version and how to reapply it; schedule the change in a maintenance window early in the week with time to recover; reassess the risk level as Medium.

Noted: recommended an offsite copy of the backups, in line with the 3-2-1 rule.

Look Back

  1. Return to what you wrote about saying no to a peer. Did it affect your review? How do you push back on a colleague and keep the relationship?
  2. "Completed" and "restorable" are different words. Where else do you accept a green checkmark without checking what it means?
  3. Why is Friday afternoon such a poor time for a change? Who pays the price if it goes wrong?
  4. Write the first two lines of the rollback plan you would want. Make them specific enough that someone else could follow them.
