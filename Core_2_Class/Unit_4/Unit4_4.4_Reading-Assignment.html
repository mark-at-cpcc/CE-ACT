Why Chapter 9 Sits With Security

The book places safety and environmental concerns in their own chapter near the end. This course reads it alongside security because the two are the same habit of mind: preventing damage before it happens. Incident response and regulated data in particular sit far more naturally beside malware and social engineering than beside documentation.

Two Performance-Based Questions This Unit

Both assigned chapters close with one — work both. That makes three of the ten you have now done, and Unit 6 works all ten together.

Objective 2.7 is a "Given a scenario" objective, so expect performance-based items there. Objectives 2.1, 2.4, 2.5, 2.9, 4.5, and 4.6 ask you to summarize, compare, and explain, which produces recognition questions — which attack is this, which method fits this situation.

Note-Taking That Earns Its Time

This unit is the largest vocabulary load of the course. Copying definitions will not survive it.

  • Sort, do not list. Six groups: physical controls, logical controls, malware types, social engineering attacks, threats, vulnerabilities. Every new term goes into a group as you meet it.
  • One symptom per malware type. "What would the user actually notice?" — files renamed and a ransom note, a fan running constantly, ads appearing everywhere. Scenario questions describe symptoms, not definitions.
  • Attack, then control. For every attack, write the one control that best stops it. Tailgating and an access control vestibule. Dictionary attacks and failed-attempt lockout. Shoulder surfing and a privacy screen.
  • Watch the pairs that look alike. Brute force and dictionary. DoS and DDoS. Phishing, vishing, smishing, spear phishing, and whaling. Degaussing and shredding. Surge suppressor and UPS. The exam lives in these distinctions.
  • Write the question, not the answer. "Which disposal method does not work on a solid-state drive?" on one side, degaussing on the other.
A Note on the Book's Index

If you look up business email compromise in the index, you will find it printed as "BEC (NEC)" — a typo in the book. The correct expansion is Business Email Compromise, and that is what the exam uses. The page reference itself is correct.
