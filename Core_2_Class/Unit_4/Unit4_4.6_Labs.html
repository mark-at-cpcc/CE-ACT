Every class opens with a Ticket of the Day. After that come two optional labs we run in class as time permits, and one take-home lab in two parts. None of these is graded. All of them are where this unit's reading turns into judgment you can actually use.

This Unit's Labs Need No Equipment

Security is the one unit where the most valuable practice is looking carefully rather than configuring something. Both classroom labs and the first take-home part use your eyes and your own machine; the second take-home part uses your judgment. That is not a shortcut — recognizing a threat and choosing a control is exactly what Domain 2.0 tests.

One per class meeting · On your own · About 20 minutes each · Section 4.11

Each class meeting has one help-desk ticket to work on your own. All three are in Section 4.11, Unit 4 Tickets, with instructions at the top of that page.

  • Ticket 1 — Tuesday: The Invoice Nobody Sent
  • Ticket 2 — Wednesday: The Clinic Walk-Through
  • Ticket 3 — Thursday: Clearing Out the Storage Room
Security Tickets Are Written Differently

When a ticket involves a possible security incident, your notes may become evidence. Record what you observed and when, in the order you observed it, and avoid speculation about who did it or why. "Found an unrecognized process running as the local administrator account at 10:42" belongs in a ticket. "Looks like the night shift installed something" does not.

Lab 4.6.1 — Spot the Phish

Tuesday · In class · 45 minutes · Objective 2.5

You will work in pairs with a printed packet of messages — emails, text messages, and QR codes. Some are legitimate. Some are not. Nothing in the packet is live, so nothing can be clicked by accident.

  1. Sort first, explain second. Go through the packet and split it into legitimate and suspicious. Do not discuss your reasoning yet — sort on instinct and note how confident you were about each one.
  2. Name the variant. For each message you flagged, identify which attack it is: phishing, vishing, smishing, QR code phishing, spear phishing, or whaling.
  3. Find the tell. For each one, write down the specific detail that gave it away — the sender address, the urgency, the mismatch between the display name and the address, the request itself.
  4. Check your misses. Trade packets with another pair and compare sorts. The messages people disagree about are the most useful ones in the room.
  5. The hard one. At least one message in the packet is legitimate but looks suspicious, and at least one is malicious with nothing obviously wrong. Find them, and say what a technician should do when the tells run out.

The Answer to Step 5 Is a Procedure, Not a Sharper Eye

A well-made business email compromise message has no spelling errors, comes from a real address, and refers to a real project. No amount of staring will catch it.

What catches it is a rule that does not depend on judgment: payment and bank-detail changes are verified by phone, using a number already on file, every time. Teach that rule to your users, and the sophistication of the message stops mattering.

Lab 4.6.2 — Physical Security Walk

Wednesday · In class · 45 minutes · Objective 2.1

An observe-only audit of the floor we are on. You will walk the public areas of the building as a group, looking at what is there and writing it down.

Observe Only — Read This Before You Leave the Room
  • Do not open any door that is closed, locked, or badge-controlled
  • Do not test whether a control works — noting that a door exists is the assignment; trying the handle is not
  • Do not photograph anything
  • Do not enter any space you would not normally enter as a student
  • Do not interact with staff or equipment; if someone asks what you are doing, say you are on a class exercise and refer them to your instructor

Probing a control without written authorization is how a security exercise becomes a security incident. That boundary is itself part of the lesson.

  1. Inventory what you can see. Walk the floor and list every physical control you observe — door locks, badge readers, cameras, lighting, equipment locks, fences or barriers outside, signage.
  2. Classify each one. Is it physical security, physical access security, or neither? Use the categories from Section 4.3.
  3. Name what each one protects against. One line per control. A camera deters and records; a cable lock stops opportunistic theft; a vestibule prevents tailgating.
  4. Find the gap — on paper. Where would a control help that you do not see one? Describe it as a recommendation, the way you would to a manager.
  5. Report out. Compare lists as a class. Different pairs notice different things, and that is the point — a real security review is done by more than one person.

Lab 4.6.3 — Take-Home, Part A: Harden Your Own Machine

On your own · 45–60 minutes · Ungraded · Objective 2.7

Unit 2 asked what your machine was doing. Unit 3 asked where its traffic went. This unit asks how hard it would be to get into.

This is an audit, not a rebuild. You are recording what you find. Change something only if you want to, and only where the step says so.

Step 1 — Who Are You When You Sign In?

  1. Check your account type. Is your everyday account a standard user or an administrator? Record which, and run whoami /groups to confirm.
  2. Check the other accounts. Run net user and record every account on the machine. Is the guest account present, and is it enabled?
  3. Check your sign-in method. Password, PIN, fingerprint, or facial recognition? Record which, and whether more than one is available.

Step 2 — The Audit

Record the current state of each item. For each one, write one line on what an attacker gains if it is weak.

  1. Screen lock timeout — how long before the machine locks itself, and does it require a credential to return?
  2. Password practice — without writing the password down, answer honestly: is it used anywhere else, and roughly how long is it?
  3. Data-at-rest encryption — is the drive encrypted? Record how you determined this.
  4. AutoRun — what happens when you insert removable media?
  5. Firewall and antivirus — are both on, and are the definitions current?
  6. Updates — when were operating system updates last installed?
  7. Firmware password — is a BIOS or UEFI password set? Do not set one for this lab; a forgotten firmware password can make a machine unusable.

Step 3 — The Write-Up

One page, in the objective 4.1 ticket format. Your resolution answers one question: if this machine were stolen from a car tonight, what would the thief be able to reach, and which single change would most reduce that?

Optional: make that one change, and record what you changed and why.

Honest Findings Are the Useful Ones

Nobody reads this but you, and a clean audit teaches nothing. If your password is reused, write that down — understanding why uniqueness matters is worth more than a tidy sheet. This is the same honesty a real security assessment requires, which is why organizations pay outsiders to do it.

Lab 4.6.4 — Take-Home, Part B: Disposal Decision Memo

On your own · 30–45 minutes · Ungraded · Objectives 2.9, 4.5

Five things need to leave an organization. For each one, decide what happens to it and defend the decision in two or three sentences. There is a defensible answer for each; there is also a wrong one.

#The SituationDecide
1Twelve working laptops, four years old, being donated to a local nonprofit. They held ordinary office documents.Which disposal or sanitization method, and why does the organization's choice differ from simply formatting?
2A failed hard drive from a medical office. It will not spin up. It held patient records.Which method, and what documentation should the organization receive afterward?
3A company smartphone returned by a departing employee, to be reissued to a new hire.What must happen before reissue, and which objective 2.8 control makes this straightforward?
4A leased multifunction copier going back to the vendor at the end of its term.What is the risk most people miss here, and what should be done about it?
5A failed UPS with a swollen battery, plus a box of used printer toner cartridges.Which document governs disposal, and what does it tell you?

Write It Up

One page, as a short memo to a manager rather than a ticket: a heading for each item, your decision, and your reasoning. Where cost and security pull in opposite directions, say so — that tension is real, and naming it is what makes a recommendation credible.

Why Item 4 Is There

Every one of these is a real disposal scenario. Item 4 is the one most people get wrong, and the reason is worth finding for yourself in Chapter 5 rather than being told here. When you find it, you will not forget it — and you will check for it on every leased device you ever handle.

What These Labs Give You

  • Practice naming a social engineering variant from the message itself, and knowing when no amount of looking is enough
  • A classified inventory of the physical controls in a building you use every week
  • An honest hardening audit of your own machine, and one change worth making
  • Five defended disposal decisions, including the leased-device risk most technicians miss
  • The professional boundary between observing a control and testing one
