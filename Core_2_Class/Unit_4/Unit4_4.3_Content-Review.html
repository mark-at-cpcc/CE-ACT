Part 1 — Security Measures (Objective 2.1)

In Plain Terms

Security comes in three layers. Physical security keeps people away from the equipment. Physical access security controls which people are allowed past a barrier. Logical security controls what a person may do once they are at a keyboard. An attacker only needs one layer to fail.

Physical Security

ControlWhat It Does
BollardsPosts that stop a vehicle from reaching a building or entrance
Access control vestibuleA two-door entry where only one door opens at a time, so one badge admits one person
Badge readerGrants entry to a credential rather than to a key
Video surveillanceRecords activity; deters, and provides evidence afterward
Alarm systemsSignal an intrusion or an environmental problem
Motion sensorsDetect movement in an area that should be empty
Door locks and equipment locksSecure rooms, and secure devices such as laptops to a desk
Security guardsApply judgment where a device cannot
FencesDefine the perimeter and slow an intruder down

Physical Access Security

These are the credentials a person carries or is: key fobs, smart cards, a mobile digital key, ordinary keys, and biometrics — retina scanner, fingerprint scanner, palm print scanner, facial recognition technology, and voice recognition technology. Lighting supports all of them by making people and cameras able to see, and magnetometers detect metal a person is carrying.

Logical Security

ConceptWhat It Means
Principle of least privilegeGive each account the minimum access its job requires, and nothing more
Zero Trust modelTrust nothing by default; verify every request regardless of where it comes from
Access control lists (ACLs)Explicit lists of who may do what to a resource
Multifactor authentication (MFA)Two or more different kinds of proof — email, hardware token, authenticator application, SMS, voice call, TOTP, or OTP
SAML and single sign-on (SSO)One authentication that grants access to many systems
Just-in-time access and PAMElevated rights granted only for the moment they are needed, then withdrawn
MDMCentral management of mobile devices and what they may do
DLPWatches for sensitive data leaving the organization and blocks it
IAM and directory servicesThe systems that hold identities and decide what each one may access

Least Privilege Is the One You Will Actually Apply

Most of this list is designed by someone else. Least privilege is a decision technicians make constantly — whether to give a user local administrator rights so a program will install, whether a shared folder needs write access or read access.

The honest tension: least privilege creates help-desk tickets. It is still right. Most ransomware that encrypts a whole file server does so because the account that opened the attachment had more access than the job needed.

Part 2 — Malware (Objective 2.4)

TypeWhat Makes It Distinct
VirusAttaches to a file or program and spreads when that file runs
TrojanDisguised as something desirable so the user installs it willingly
RootkitHides itself deep in the operating system to avoid detection
SpywareCollects information about the user and sends it elsewhere
RansomwareEncrypts files and demands payment for the key
KeyloggerRecords keystrokes to capture passwords and other typed data
Boot sector virusInfects the startup code so it loads before the operating system
CryptominerUses the machine's processor to mine cryptocurrency for someone else
StalkerwareMonitors a specific person's device activity and location, usually installed by someone who knows them
FilelessRuns in memory using built-in tools, leaving little on disk for a scanner to find

Tools and Methods

  • Antivirus and anti-malware — detection and removal on the endpoint
  • EDR, MDR, and XDR — endpoint, managed, and extended detection and response: monitoring that watches behavior rather than matching known signatures
  • Email security gateway — filters malicious mail before it reaches the user
  • Software firewalls — control what a compromised machine can talk to
  • Recovery console — a repair environment for a machine that will not start cleanly
  • OS reinstallation — the certain answer when removal cannot be trusted
  • User education regarding common threats, including antiphishing training — the control with the best return of anything on this list
A Real Example You Will Meet on the Job

A user's machine is slow, the fan runs constantly, and Task Manager shows a process at 95% CPU that the user does not recognize. Nothing is encrypted and nothing is being stolen — the machine is mining cryptocurrency for someone else. Cryptominers are common precisely because they do not announce themselves; the only symptom is a computer that is inexplicably busy. Your Unit 2 skills found it.

Removal Comes in Unit 6

This unit is about recognizing malware. CompTIA's ten-step malware removal procedure is objective 2.6, and it is taught in Unit 6 with the rest of troubleshooting. For now, learn what each type does and which tool addresses it.

Part 3 — Social Engineering, Threats, and Vulnerabilities (Objective 2.5)

Social Engineering — Attacking the Person

AttackHow It Works
PhishingA fraudulent message that appears legitimate, aiming to collect credentials or deliver malware
VishingPhishing by voice call
SmishingPhishing by text message
QR code phishingA malicious link hidden in a QR code, which a person cannot inspect before scanning
Spear phishingPhishing tailored to a specific person using details about them
WhalingSpear phishing aimed at an executive
Shoulder surfingReading a screen or keyboard over someone's shoulder
TailgatingFollowing an authorized person through a controlled door
ImpersonationPretending to be someone with a right to access — often "IT support"
Dumpster divingRecovering useful information from discarded paper and equipment

Impersonation Is About You

The most effective impersonation script in the world is "Hi, this is IT — I need your password to fix your account." It works because technicians really do call users, and users want to be helpful.

Two habits protect your users: never ask for a password, ever, so that anyone who does is immediately suspect; and tell users plainly, on the first call you make to them, that you will never ask. That one sentence does more for an organization's security than most software.

Vulnerabilities

A threat is what someone might do; a vulnerability is the opening that lets them. CompTIA names five: non-compliant systems, unpatched systems, unprotected systems (missing antivirus or firewall), end-of-life software no longer receiving fixes, and bring your own device, where personal equipment the organization does not control holds its data.

A Real Example You Will Meet on the Job

An accounts-payable clerk receives an email from the company's real domain, from a real colleague's address, referring to a real project, asking for a vendor's bank details to be updated before Friday's run. Nothing is misspelled and no attachment is involved — the colleague's mailbox was compromised weeks ago. This is business email compromise, and it is why "check the sender address" is not enough advice. The control that stops it is a procedure: bank detail changes are verified by phone, using a number already on file.

Part 4 — Hardening the Workstation (Objective 2.7)

Everything so far has been recognition. This objective is the first set of defenses you apply yourself, and Unit 5 continues it.

Passwords and Encryption

Data-at-rest encryption protects information on a drive that is powered off — the control that makes a stolen laptop a lost asset rather than a data breach.

CompTIA names five password considerations: length, character types, uniqueness, complexity, and expiration. Length does the most work; a long passphrase resists brute force better than a short password with symbols substituted for letters. BIOS/UEFI passwords protect the firmware itself, so an attacker cannot simply change the boot order and start from their own media.

Account Management

  • Restrict user permissions — least privilege, applied to accounts
  • Restrict log-in times — a warehouse account that only works during shift hours cannot be used at 3 a.m.
  • Disable the guest account — an unmonitored way in
  • Use failed attempts lockout — the control that defeats brute-force and dictionary attacks
  • Use timeout and screen lock — protects an unattended desk
  • Apply account expiration dates — contractor accounts that close themselves

System Hardening

Change the default administrator account and password — default credentials remain one of the most reliable ways into a network. Disable AutoRun so inserted media cannot execute on its own. Disable unused services so that software nobody uses cannot be attacked.

End-User Best Practices

These are what you teach users: use screensaver locks, log off when not in use, secure and protect critical hardware such as laptops, secure personally identifiable information and passwords, and use password managers.

Why Password Managers Are on a Certification Exam

Uniqueness is the password rule people break, because nobody can remember forty different passwords. So they reuse one, and a breach at any site becomes a breach everywhere.

A password manager is the only practical way an ordinary person achieves uniqueness across every account. Recommending one is real security advice, not a product pitch — and it is why CompTIA lists it as an end-user best practice.

Part 5 — Data Destruction and Disposal (Objective 2.9)

A drive that leaves your control still holding data is a breach that has not been discovered yet. There are two paths: destroy the drive, or clear it for reuse.

MethodWhat It DoesWhen to Use It
DrillingPhysically punctures the plattersQuick on-site destruction of a small number of drives
ShreddingReduces the drive to fragmentsBulk destruction, usually by a vendor
DegaussingDestroys the magnetic data with a strong fieldMagnetic hard drives. It does not work on solid-state drives.
IncinerationBurns the mediaHigh-security environments, by a certified vendor
Erasing or wipingOverwrites the data so the drive can be reusedRedeploying a machine inside the organization, or donating it
Low-level formattingResets the drive's structures at the factory levelRare in practice on modern drives
Standard formattingPrepares a filesystem — it does not reliably destroy dataPreparing a drive for use, never for secure disposal

When a vendor does the work, outsourcing concepts apply: a third-party vendor performs the destruction and provides a certification of destruction or recycling — your proof that the data is gone. Regulatory and environmental requirements govern both the data and the electronic waste.

Formatting Is Not Destruction

A standard format removes the filesystem's index, not the data. Freely available tools recover from formatted drives routinely. If the question involves a drive leaving the organization and the data is sensitive, the answer is destruction or a verified wipe — never "format it."

Part 6 — Safety, Environment, and Policy (Objectives 4.4, 4.5, 4.6)

Safety Procedures (4.4)

You met electrostatic discharge in Core 1; it returns here because it belongs to the technician's professional practice, not just to hardware. ESD straps, ESD mats, and antistatic bags control static. Electrical safety includes equipment grounding. Proper component handling and storage and cable management prevent damage and trip hazards, and technicians work in compliance with government regulations.

Personal safety is the half people skip: disconnect power before repairing a PC, use proper lifting techniques, observe fire safety, and wear safety goggles and an air filter mask when the work calls for them.

Connect It to Core 1

In Core 1 ESD was about protecting the component. Here it is about the procedure you follow every time, whether or not you think this particular job is risky. The difference between a technician and someone who is good with computers is that the technician does it the same way on the boring jobs too.

Environmental Controls (4.5)

  • MSDS documentation — the material safety data sheet tells you how to handle and dispose of a substance, including proper battery disposal, proper toner disposal, and proper disposal of other devices and assets
  • Temperature, humidity awareness, and proper ventilation — including location and equipment placement, dust cleanup, and the use of compressed air and vacuums
  • Power surges, brownouts, and blackouts — a surge suppressor absorbs a spike; an uninterruptible power supply carries equipment through a sag or a short outage so it can shut down in an orderly way

Surge Suppressor or UPS?

A scenario question will describe one of two situations. If the concern is a spike — lightning, a large motor switching on — the answer is a surge suppressor. If the concern is power going away, even briefly, and equipment needing to stay up or shut down cleanly, the answer is a UPS. A UPS also suppresses surges; a surge suppressor does nothing when the power stops.

Policy, Licensing, and Privacy (4.6)

Incident response is what a technician does when they find something that should not be there:

  • Chain of custody — documenting everyone who handled the evidence and when
  • Informing management or law enforcement as necessary — escalate; do not investigate alone
  • Copy of drive — work from an image to preserve data integrity
  • Incident documentation — what was found, when, by whom
  • Order of volatility — collect the evidence that disappears first, such as memory contents, before the evidence that persists

Licensing covers DRM and the EULA, valid licenses, perpetual license agreements, the difference between a personal-use and a corporate-use license, and open-source licenses. An NDA or MNDA governs confidential information.

Regulated data gets special handling: credit card payment information, personal government-issued information, PII, healthcare data, and data retention requirements. An acceptable use policy defines what users may do with company systems, and regulatory and business compliance requirements — including splash screens that state terms before access — round out the objective.

The Hardest Part of This Job Is in This Objective

Sooner or later a technician working on a machine finds something they wish they had not. The rule is the same every time: stop, preserve, and escalate. Do not open more files to be certain. Do not delete anything. Do not tell a colleague. Follow your organization's incident response procedure, document what you did, and hand it to the people whose job it is. Knowing this before it happens is the entire reason the objective exists.

Unit 4 Content Recap

  • Security has three layers: physical, physical access, and logical — an attacker needs only one to fail
  • Least privilege is the logical control technicians apply most often, and the one that limits the damage of everything else
  • Malware types differ by what they do: encrypt, record, hide, spy, mine, or advertise
  • Detection ranges from signature-based antivirus to behavior-based EDR, MDR, and XDR — and user education outperforms most of it
  • Social engineering attacks the person: phishing and its variants, shoulder surfing, tailgating, impersonation, and dumpster diving
  • A threat is what someone might do; a vulnerability is the opening that lets them
  • Hardening is passwords, account management, disabling what is unused, and teaching users
  • Standard formatting does not destroy data; wiping, drilling, shredding, degaussing, and incineration do
  • Personal safety matters as much as equipment safety — disconnect power, lift properly, wear protection
  • A surge suppressor handles spikes; a UPS handles power going away
  • When you find something that should not be there: stop, preserve, escalate, document
