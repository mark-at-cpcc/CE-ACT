Three optional labs we run in class as time permits, and one take-home lab you do on your own machine. None of these is graded. All of them are where this week's reading turns into something you can actually do. Every class also opens with a Ticket of the Day — that section is at the end of this page.

Ungraded Does Not Mean Optional Learning

The labs in this section carry no points. They carry something better: the first time you do a thing is the first time you really understand it. Students who skip the hands-on work can usually recite the difference between FAT32 and exFAT. Students who do it can pick the right one under exam pressure.

Before You Touch Anything — Data Safety

Core 2 work can destroy data faster than a screwdriver ever could. Formatting, partitioning, and installation are permanent operations.

  • Format only drives supplied for the lab or USB drives you own and have emptied
  • Never run a format, partition, or installation on a machine holding data you need
  • Assume anything on a lab machine will be wiped — save nothing there
  • Classroom hardware rules from Core 1 still apply: unplug before opening a case, wear the strap, and no power supply is opened by anyone, ever

Lab 1.6.1 — Tier 1 and Tier 2 Support: Roles, Tickets, and the Handoff

Thursday · In class · 45–60 minutes · Objectives 4.1, 4.7

Purpose

Your first IT job will almost certainly be a Tier 1 or Tier 2 support role, remote or in person. This lab shows you what each role actually does, what a ticket looks like from the inside, and how a problem moves from one tier to the next without being dropped. Read Parts A through C, then complete the practice in Part D.

The Support Tiers at a Glance

Most help desks organize support in levels, with each level taking the problems the level below it cannot resolve. The goal is simple: solve the common problems quickly at the first point of contact, and send the hard ones to people with the depth to fix them — with nothing lost in the handoff.

TierWhoTypical WorkEscalates To
Tier 0Self-serviceKnowledge base articles, password reset portals, chatbotsTier 1
Tier 1Help desk or service desk technician — the first human contactLogging and categorizing tickets, password resets after identity verification, account lockouts, basic connectivity, email and printer setup, known fixes from the knowledge baseTier 2
Tier 2Experienced technician or desktop support specialistDeeper diagnosis, driver and software configuration, hardware replacement, problems that need administrative access or a site visitTier 3, or the vendor
Tier 3Engineers, administrators, or the vendorServer, network, and application problems that need design-level knowledge or a code fixVendor or development team

Remote or In Person

Both tiers exist in both settings, and the setting changes how the work is done more than what the work is.

RemoteIn Person
Tier 1Phone, chat, email, and portal tickets. Guides the user through steps, or connects with a remote support tool after the user agrees. Most of the volume lives here.A walk-up counter or service desk at a school, hospital, or office. Same tickets, plus loaner equipment, badge or account issues handled face to face.
Tier 2Advanced remote tools: remote monitoring and management, administrative consoles, log review. Works the escalations Tier 1 could not close.Desktop support or field technicians. Part A — Best Practices in Each Role

Tier 1 — first contact.

  • Verify identity before you change anything. A password reset for the wrong person is a security incident, and impersonating a user to the help desk is one of the oldest social engineering attacks there is. Follow your organization's verification procedure every time, even when the caller sounds rushed or important.
  • Log the contact as it happens. Open the ticket at the start of the call, not after it. Notes written from memory an hour later lose the details that matter.
  • Gather before you fix. Who is affected, what exactly is happening, when it started, what changed, and what the user has already tried. "What changed?" finds more causes than any tool.
  • Prioritize by impact and urgency, not by who called first or who is loudest. One person who cannot print is not the same as a department that cannot work.
  • Use the knowledge base first. Most Tier 1 problems have been solved before. Following the documented fix is faster and safer than improvising.
  • Set expectations honestly. Tell the user what happens next and roughly when. "I'm escalating this to desktop support; you should hear from them within two hours" beats a vague "someone will get back to you."
  • Escalate early, and completely. When the problem is outside your scope, permissions, or time limit, hand it off with everything you learned — so the next technician never has to ask the user to repeat themselves.
  • Never close a ticket the user has not confirmed is resolved.

Tier 2 — the escalation.

  • Read the full ticket before you contact the user. Asking questions Tier 1 already asked tells the user nobody is paying attention.
  • Reproduce the problem, then test one change at a time. If you cannot make it happen, you cannot prove you fixed it.
  • Find the cause, not just a workaround. Record the root cause in the ticket, not only what made the symptom go away.
  • Follow change management for anything that affects more than one machine or touches production systems.
  • Teach the fix back down. Write or update the knowledge base article so Tier 1 can resolve the next one without escalating. Good Tier 2 work makes Tier 1 more capable over time.
  • Escalate to Tier 3 or the vendor with evidence — logs, error messages, versions, and what you have ruled out.

Remote work, specifically.

  • Ask permission before starting a remote session, and say what you are about to do.
  • Do not open files, email, or windows unrelated to the problem. If personal information is on screen, ask the user to close it.
  • On the phone, describe what the user should see before asking them to click — "you should see a blue button labeled Restart."
  • End the session clearly and confirm the remote connection is closed.

In-person work, specifically.

  • Arrive when you said you would; if you will be late, call ahead.
  • Respect the workspace. Ask before moving personal items, and do not read documents on the desk.
  • Keep the user's screen private while you work, and lock it if you step away.
  • Before you leave, explain what you did in plain language and confirm the problem is gone.

Part B — The Anatomy of a Ticket

A ticket is the official record of a problem from the moment it is reported to the moment it is closed. Every field exists so that someone who was not there can understand what happened.

How priority is set. Most desks combine impact (how much of the business is affected) with urgency (how quickly it hurts) to reach a priority, instead of judging by feel:

Impact ↓ / Urgency →LowMediumHigh
High — many users or a critical serviceMediumHighCritical
Medium — a team or an important functionLowMediumHigh
Low — one user, a workaround existsLowLowMedium

How a ticket moves. Nearly every help desk follows the same path:

  1. Log — the ticket is created with the requester, device, channel, and description.
  2. Categorize and prioritize — the category routes it; impact and urgency set its place in the queue.
  3. Assign — a person or group takes ownership.
  4. Investigate — the troubleshooting method you learned in Core 1, recorded as progress notes.
  5. Escalate, if needed — with everything learned so far.
  6. Resolve — the fix is applied and the root cause recorded.
  7. Confirm and close — the user agrees it is fixed; the ticket closes; anything worth sharing becomes a knowledge base article.

What Made This a Good Ticket

Tier 1 confirmed the problem was one laptop, not the network, before doing anything else. They solved the user's real problem — the client call — with a workaround while the actual fault waited for the right person. The escalation note told Tier 2 exactly what had been tried, so Tier 2 walked in already knowing where to look. And Tier 2 did not stop at the fix: they recorded the root cause and changed the knowledge base, so the next technician who sees this resolves it at Tier 1.

Part D — Practice

Work on your own. Use this call:

The Call

"Hi, this is Robert in Payroll. I can't print to the second-floor printer. It just sits in the queue. Everybody else in Payroll seems fine, and I really need to print checks by noon."

  1. Decide who you are. You are Tier 1, working remotely by phone. Write down the first three questions you would ask, in order.
  2. Build the ticket header. Fill in requester, device (invent reasonable details), channel, category, and a description with Robert's words and your summary.
  3. Set the priority with the impact-and-urgency table, and explain your choice in one sentence.
  4. Write two progress notes for the Tier 1 steps you would try first.
  5. Decide: resolve or escalate. Assume your steps did not work. Write the escalation note to Tier 2 desktop support — detailed enough that they never have to call Robert to ask what happened.
  6. Write what you would tell Robert before you hang up, so he knows what happens next and when.
Check Your Work

Read your escalation note as if you were the Tier 2 technician who just received it. Could you walk to Robert's desk and start working without asking him a single question? If not, add what is missing. Then compare your ticket with the anatomy table in Part B: every field should be filled in.

Lab 1.6.2 — File System Face-Off

Wednesday · In class · 45–60 minutes · Objective 1.1

Purpose

Format the same drive three ways — FAT32, exFAT, and NTFS — copy the same oversized file each time, and record what changes. By the end you will be able to choose the right file system for a drive and explain why, from evidence you produced yourself.

Why This Method

This lab is a controlled comparison: one thing changes and everything else stays the same. The drive, the computer, and the test file are identical in every round. Only the file system changes. That design matters for four reasons.

  • It isolates the cause. When the copy fails on FAT32 and succeeds on exFAT with the same drive and the same file, the file system is the only possible explanation. This is the same logic as step 3 of the troubleshooting method — test the theory by changing one thing at a time. You are practicing it on purpose.
  • It produces the symptom you will meet on the job. Customers do not report "a FAT32 single-file size limit." They report "it says the file is too large, but the drive is empty." Seeing that exact error once means you recognize it instantly later — and the exam describes symptoms the same way.
  • It turns a memorized fact into a judgment. Knowing that FAT32 cannot hold a file over 4 GB is recall. Choosing exFAT for a drive shared between a PC and a Mac, and NTFS for files that need permissions, is the skill. Your recommendations at the end come from your own results.
  • It is safe and repeatable. You use an empty lab drive and a test file generated for the purpose, so no real data is ever at risk, and any classmate can repeat your steps and get the same result.

Materials

  • A Windows lab computer you can sign in to as an administrator
  • An empty USB flash drive of 32 GB or smaller, issued for the lab. Windows' built-in formatting tools will not offer FAT32 for larger drives.
  • At least 6 GB of free space on the computer's own drive, for the test file
  • Optional: a Mac, for the cross-platform test in step 9
Before You Begin

Formatting erases everything on the drive. Confirm the USB drive is the lab drive and is empty, and double-check the drive letter before every format. Formatting the wrong drive is the most common way people lose data on purpose.

Procedure

  1. Create the test file. Open Command Prompt as administrator, then run mkdir C:\Lab followed by fsutil file createnew C:\Lab\testfile.bin 5368709120. This creates an empty file of exactly 5 GB — larger than FAT32 can hold — without needing a real video. Confirm its size in File Explorer.
  2. Identify the USB drive. Insert it, open File Explorer, and note its drive letter and capacity. Open its Properties and confirm it is empty.
  3. Round 1: format as FAT32. Right-click the drive, choose Format, select FAT32, leave Quick Format checked, and start. Record the file system options offered, the default allocation unit size, and how long the format took.
  4. Copy the test file. Copy testfile.bin to the drive. Record the exact error message, word for word, and the drive's free space at the moment the copy failed.
  5. Round 2: format as exFAT using the same steps, then copy the same test file. Record whether it succeeded and how long it took.
  6. Round 3: format as NTFS, then copy the same test file. Record the result.
  7. Inspect what NTFS adds. Right-click the copied file, choose Properties, and look for the Security tab. Record what you find. Then check the FAT32 and exFAT results from your notes: did those rounds have a Security tab?
  8. Fill in the results table below from your notes.
  9. Optional cross-platform test. If a Mac is available, connect the drive after each format and record whether the Mac can read it, write to it, or both.
  10. Clean up. Delete C:\Lab\testfile.bin, and return the USB drive formatted as exFAT, empty.

Results Table

Copy this into your notes and complete it from your own observations.

CheckFAT32exFATNTFS
5 GB file copied?

Analysis: Make the Recommendation

Using only your results table, recommend a file system for each scenario and give the one result that decided it:

  1. A flash drive shared between a Windows PC and a Mac, holding video files larger than 4 GB.
  2. An internal drive holding company files that must allow some employees access and deny it to others.
  3. A flash drive of music for a car stereo that only supports older formats.
Check Your Work

Your recommendations should be exFAT for the shared drive, NTFS for the company files, and FAT32 for the car stereo — with each justified by something you saw, not something you remembered. If your evidence pointed somewhere else, look at the step where it diverged; that is the most useful thing you will learn today.

Lab 1.6.3 — Will It Run? Minimum Versus Recommended Requirements

Thursday · In class · 45–60 minutes · Objectives 1.10, 1.3

Purpose

"Will this run on my computer?" is one of the questions customers ask most. A professional answer is never just yes or no. It says whether the software will install, how well it will run, and which specific part of the computer decides that. This lab teaches you to read both sets of requirements and give that answer.

What the Two Numbers Mean

Minimum requirements

The lowest configuration on which the vendor says the software will install and run in a supported way. Meeting the minimum means it works — often slowly, with features turned down, or only for light work.

Recommended requirements

The configuration the vendor expects to deliver the intended experience for typical work. Meeting the recommended specifications means it runs the way the vendor designed it to.

How the Two Relate

Minimum and recommended are not two different answers to the same question. They describe the same software at two levels of performance. Between them is a range where the software runs, but with limits the user will notice. That gives you three possible verdicts, not two:

  • Below minimum — may not install, or installs unsupported. Vendor support may decline to help.
  • Between minimum and recommended — runs, with limits. Your job is to name the limit: slow previews, lower settings, smaller projects.
  • At or above recommended — runs as intended.

When a customer is buying a computer, recommend at least the recommended specifications. A machine bought at the minimum is at the minimum on its first day and below it after the next software update.

Three Real-World Examples

Each example below was taken from the vendor's own published requirements when this course was prepared. Vendors change these figures — one of the three changed in 2026 — so always check the vendor's current page before you advise anyone.

What the Three Examples Teach Together
  • Recommended is tied to a workload or performance target — HD versus 4K, 30 versus 60 frames per second.
  • Memory and graphics are usually where the gap is largest. Check those two lines first.
  • Some requirements are features, not sizes. AVX2, DirectX 12, and Vulkan 1.3 are yes-or-no. A computer with plenty of memory can still fail on a feature.
  • Requirements belong to a specific version. "Will AutoCAD run?" has no answer until you know which AutoCAD.

Best Practices for Requirements Research

  • Use the vendor's official page for the exact product and version. Third-party summary sites often disagree with each other and with the vendor; while this lab was being prepared, different sites listed different memory and graphics figures for the same software.
  • Record the date you checked and the version, so your answer can be verified later.
  • Check every line, not just memory. Operating system version, processor features, graphics memory, free storage, and display resolution can each block an installation.
  • Ask what the person will actually do with it. Light use and heavy use have different answers on the same computer.
  • Read the computer's specifications from the computer, using the tools below — not from memory, a sales sticker, or what the user believes it has.
  • Document your answer the way you would in a ticket: the requirement, the computer's value, and the verdict.

Step 2 — Research and Compare: Minimum Requirements First

Always start with the minimum. If the computer fails it, the recommended comparison does not matter yet.

  1. Find the vendor's official requirements page for the exact product and version, and record the date.
  2. Copy the minimum requirements line by line into the Minimum column of the comparison table below.
  3. Enter the computer's value for each line from Step 1.
  4. Mark each line Meets or Fails. Treat feature requirements, such as DirectX 12, as yes or no.
  5. Reach the minimum verdict. If any line fails, the verdict is below minimum — will not install, or unsupported. Name the failing requirement. If every line meets, continue to Step 3.

Step 3 — Repeat the Process for Recommended Requirements

  1. Return to the same vendor page and copy the recommended requirements line by line into the Recommended column.
  2. Compare each line with the computer's values from Step 1, and mark Meets or Falls short.
  3. Reach the final verdict. If every line meets, the verdict is runs as intended. If any line falls short, the verdict is runs, with limits — name the line and describe what the user will notice.
  4. Write the answer in one or two sentences a customer would understand, naming the specific requirement that decided it.

Lab 1.6.4 — Take-Home: Document Your Own Machine

On your own · 1–1.5 hours · Ungraded · Objectives 1.1, 1.3, 1.10, 4.1

Purpose

Every technician should be able to inventory a computer in ten minutes. This week you do it on the machine you know best — your own — and write it up the way a help desk would.

Nothing in this lab changes a setting. Every step is read-only: you are looking, not configuring.

Procedure

  1. Identify the operating system. Press Windows key + R, type winver, and press Enter. Record the edition and version. On macOS, use the Apple menu, then About This Mac. On Linux, run cat /etc/os-release in a terminal.
  2. Record the hardware. Open Settings, then System, then About (or the equivalent on your OS). Record the processor, installed RAM, and system type.
  3. Record storage and file system. Open File Explorer, right-click your main drive, and choose Properties. Record the file system, total capacity, and free space. Repeat for any external or USB drive you have.
  4. Check firmware and security features. Run msinfo32 and record the BIOS mode (UEFI or Legacy) and whether Secure Boot is on. Then run tpm.msc and record whether a TPM is present and its version.
  5. Judge it against Windows 11. Using the requirements in Section 1.3, state whether your machine meets each one, and name the specific requirement that would block an upgrade if any would.
  6. Check one application. Pick an application you actually want to install, find its published requirements, and state whether your machine meets them.
  7. Write it up as a ticket. One page, in the objective 4.1 format: device information, a description of what you were asked to determine, your progress notes, and your resolution — a clear recommendation in language a non-technical customer would understand.
Bring It to Class

Bring your write-up to Thursday's session. We compare machines as a group, and the range across the room — some upgradeable, some not — teaches the requirements faster than any slide.
