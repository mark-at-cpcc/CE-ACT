Unit 1 — The Technician and the Operating System

The software half of A+ starts now: what an operating system is, how Windows gets onto a machine, and how a professional technician documents the work.

Where We Are

Welcome to Unit 1 of Core 2. In Core 1 you learned what is inside the computer. In this unit you meet the software that brings that hardware to life — and the professional habits that make customers trust a technician.

Unit 1 covers two exam domains at once, on purpose. Domain 1.0 Operating Systems (objectives 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.10, and 1.11) is the foundation for every Core 2 topic that follows. Domain 4.0 Operational Procedures (objectives 4.1 and 4.7) is tickets, documentation, and communication — 21% of the exam, and the clearest picture of the job you are training for.

In Plain Terms

An operating system is the manager that sits between you and the hardware. It decides which program gets the processor, where files are stored, and what you see on the screen. In this unit you learn the main operating systems, how Windows is installed and upgraded, how to tell whether a program will run on a computer, and how to write down your work so the next technician can pick up where you stopped.

What You'll Be Able to Do by the End of This Unit

By the end of Unit 1, you will be able to:

  1. Identify the common workstation and mobile operating systems and state what each is used for
  2. Compare the file systems on the exam — NTFS, ReFS, FAT32, exFAT, ext4, XFS, and APFS — and choose the right one for a given drive
  3. Compare the features and hardware requirements of the Windows 10 and Windows 11 editions
  4. Describe Windows boot methods, installation types, partitioning schemes, and upgrade considerations
  5. Evaluate an application's system requirements and distribution method before installing it
  6. Describe how cloud-based productivity tools, file sync, and licenses are set up for users
  7. Write a help-desk ticket that clearly documents a problem, the work done, and the resolution
  8. Apply professional communication techniques when working with a customer

One Thing That Trips People Up

Windows Home Is Not a Smaller Windows Pro

Most people assume the Windows editions are the same product with a few extras added at the top. For a technician, the differences are the whole point.

Here's how it plays out on the job: a small dental office buys five laptops on sale. They arrive with Windows 11 Home. On setup day the technician finds that Home cannot join the office's domain, does not include BitLocker, and cannot accept incoming Remote Desktop connections. The office can't manage those laptops centrally, can't encrypt patient records the way its policy requires, and its IT support can't connect in remotely. The fix is an edition upgrade on every machine — time and money nobody planned to spend.

On the exam: when a question mentions a business network, a domain, BitLocker, or connecting to a machine with Remote Desktop, check the Windows edition first. The edition is often the answer.
