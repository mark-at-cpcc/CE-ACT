Welcome back — and welcome to Core 2. In Core 1 you learned what is inside the box. This week you meet the software that brings that hardware to life, and the professional habits that make a technician someone customers trust.

Nothing Is Due Before You Arrive

Our first meeting is Tuesday, the first day of the term. There is no preparation to do beforehand — no reading, no videos, no pre-work. Your diagnostics are completed together in class on Tuesday, and assigned work begins after that first session. Just show up.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Identify the common workstation and mobile operating systems and state what each is used for
  2. Compare the file systems on the exam — NTFS, ReFS, FAT32, exFAT, ext4, XFS, and APFS — and choose the right one for a given drive
  3. Compare the features and hardware requirements of the Windows 10 and Windows 11 editions
  4. Describe Windows boot methods, installation types, partitioning schemes, and upgrade considerations
  5. Evaluate an application's system requirements and distribution method before installing it
  6. Describe how cloud-based productivity tools, file sync, and licenses are set up for users
  7. Write a help-desk ticket that clearly documents a problem, the work done, and the resolution
  8. Apply professional communication techniques when working with a customer
Technical Overview

Unit 1 addresses CompTIA A+ Core 2 (220-1202) Domain 1.0 Operating Systems — objectives 1.1 (OS types, file systems, vendor life cycles), 1.2 (installations and upgrades), 1.3 (Windows editions), 1.10 (application installation), and 1.11 (cloud-based productivity tools) — together with Domain 4.0 Operational Procedures objectives 4.1 (documentation and support systems) and 4.7 (communication and professionalism). Instruction covers workstation and mobile operating system families, filesystem capabilities and limits, edition feature differences including domain membership, BitLocker, and RDP availability, boot and deployment methods, GPT and MBR partitioning, application requirement analysis, and ticketing, asset management, and documentation practice.

In Plain Terms

An operating system is the manager that sits between you and the hardware you learned in Core 1. It decides which program gets the processor, where files are stored, and what you see on the screen. This week you learn the different operating systems, how Windows gets installed and upgraded, and how to tell whether a program will run on a given computer. You also start doing the thing that separates a technician from a hobbyist: writing down what you did so the next person can follow it.

Why We Start Here

Core 2 is the software half of A+. Operating systems and security together make up 56% of the Core 2 exam, and every one of those questions assumes you know what an operating system is and how it gets onto a machine. That is this week.

We also start with Operational Procedures — tickets, documentation, and professional communication. That domain is 21% of the exam, and it is the most practical material in the course: it describes the actual job. Knowing why a technician documents everything makes the technical content that follows easier to place.

Every Class Starts With a Ticket

Each class opens with a short customer complaint — the kind a help desk hears every day, such as "My computer keeps restarting." You spend about fifteen minutes writing it up as a proper help-desk ticket: who the user is, what device, what the problem is, what you checked, and how it was resolved.

It is ungraded. It is also the single most job-like thing we do. By Week 6 you will have written eighteen tickets, and writing a clear one will feel routine — which is exactly what an employer is looking for.

How This Unit Is Graded

Three things are graded: the NETLAB labs (1.8), the reflection (1.9), and the unit test (1.10). Each is worth 100 points, awarded in full when it meets the completion standard on that item's page. Work that falls short is returned for revision and resubmitted for full credit — it is not partially scored.

Everything else in this unit is ungraded: the pre-assessment, your reading and video notes, the practice questions, the labs, and the acronym test. They are still expected, and they are where the learning actually happens — the graded items only measure it.

As in Core 1, the score that decides everything is your scaled score on practice exams. We will track it every week.

Core 2 Passes at 700, Not 675

Core 1 passed at 675 out of 900. Core 2 passes at 700. Because the scale starts at 100 rather than zero, 700 works out to about 75% of raw points — not the roughly 72% you needed in Core 1.

That difference matters. A student who passed Core 1 comfortably at 73% would fail Core 2 with the same performance. Aim higher from day one.
