Lab 6.6.1 — Diagnose a Real One

Tuesday · In class · 60 minutes · Objectives 3.1, 3.4, 4.1

Not a prepared fault with a known answer. Bring a real problem — something genuinely wrong with your own machine, a family member's, or one you have been meaning to look at — and work it properly in front of people who will question your reasoning.

What to Bring, and What Not To
  • Bring the machine if you can, or a clear written description of the symptom if you cannot
  • Bring a machine you are authorized to work on — your own, or one whose owner has agreed
  • Do not bring an employer's equipment, or any machine holding someone else's personal or client data
  • If no machine is available, your instructor will pair you with someone or supply a documented case
  1. State the symptom in the user's words first, before you say what you think it is. "It's slow" and "the browser takes ten seconds to open pages, but files open instantly" are different tickets.
  2. Establish what changed. When did it start? What happened around then — an update, an install, a new device, a move?
  3. Write down three candidate causes before touching anything, and rank them. Committing to a hypothesis in writing is what makes the next step honest.
  4. Narrow by halves. This machine or every machine? This user or every user? This application or everything? Record what each answer eliminates.
  5. Test one thing at a time, recording the result of each. If a test disproves your top candidate, say so out loud — that is the part most people skip.
  6. Choose the least disruptive fix that addresses the cause rather than the symptom, and apply it if it is safe to do so in class.
  7. Write the ticket. Device information, what you were asked to determine, progress notes in order, and a resolution a customer would understand.

Present the Ones You Did Not Solve

In the last fifteen minutes, pairs report out — and the unsolved cases are the valuable ones. Explain what you ruled out and what you would do next with more time or more access.

That is what a real escalation looks like. A technician who hands over a ticket saying "it is not the network, not the profile, and not the application — here is what I tested" has done the job, even without a fix. A technician who hands over "it is broken" has not.

Lab 6.6.2 — Check the AI, Extended

Thursday · In class · 45–60 minutes · Objective 4.10

Section 6.3 says an AI tool produces what is probable, not what is correct, and that you should use it to draft rather than to decide. This lab is where you stop taking that on trust.

  1. Choose five questions from across the A+ objectives, with at least one from each domain. Pick topics you now know well — you cannot catch an error in material you have not learned.
  2. Ask an AI tool each one, and record its answer in full, exactly as given.
  3. Verify every answer against the CompTIA objectives, your textbook, or vendor documentation. Record the source you checked against.
  4. Mark each answer correct, partly correct, or wrong — and for anything less than correct, write down precisely what was wrong with it.
  5. Push on one answer. Tell the tool its correct answer was wrong, and see whether it holds its position or changes it. Record what happened.
  6. Ask for a source, then check whether the source exists and says what the tool claimed.
  7. Report out as a class and collect every error found. The pattern across the room is the lesson.
What Step 5 Usually Shows

Tools often abandon a correct answer when challenged, because agreeing is a likely response. If a tool will change a right answer because you pushed back, it cannot be the thing that decides anything — and a technician who takes its output to a customer owns the error entirely.

Two Boundaries
  • Nothing confidential goes into the tool — no customer data, credentials, or internal documents. The data privacy risk in objective 4.10 is not hypothetical.
  • This lab is not a study method. You are testing the tool against material you already know. Using an AI as a primary source for certification study produces exactly the confident errors you are about to catch.

Ticket of the Day

One per class meeting · On your own · About 20 minutes each · Section 6.11

Each class meeting has one help-desk ticket to work on your own. All three are in Section 6.11, Unit 6 Tickets, with instructions at the top of that page.

  • Ticket 1 — Tuesday: Pop-ups, Redirects, and Updates That Will Not Install
  • Ticket 2 — Wednesday: Blue Screens After the Update
  • Ticket 3 — Thursday: Remote Help, a Strange Phone, and an AI's Advice
The Last Three

You have now written eighteen of these. Compare your Unit 1 notes with what you write this week. The difference between "checked the computer" and a note that names the tool, the finding, and the action is the difference the course was built to produce — and it is visible in your own handwriting.

What These Labs Give You

  • One real fault worked end to end, with your reasoning questioned by someone else while you worked it
  • Practice writing a ticket that is useful when you did not solve the problem
  • Documented evidence, found by you, of where an AI tool is confidently wrong
  • A tested habit of verifying before acting — the one that keeps the error from reaching a customer
