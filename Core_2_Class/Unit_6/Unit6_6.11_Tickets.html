Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three help-desk tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you? You will come back to it.
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them from the user's computer.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution, below the solution itself. Answer them in your notes. They are about your thinking, not the content, and nobody else will see them.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • If you miss one, go back to the exhibit and the call and work out why. That search is the lesson.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note. The Look Back prompts are inside it.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of the output from the computer being troubleshot — what you would see on that machine's screen if you opened the tool or settings window, or ran the command shown. Treat them as evidence you collected yourself during the call.

They are simplified to show what matters, the same way a performance-based question on the 220-1202 exam presents a tool or a command's output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA's troubleshooting method it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and implement the solution
  5. Verify full system functionality and, if applicable, implement preventive measures
  6. Document findings, actions, and outcomes

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Ticket 1 of 3 Pop-ups, Redirects, and Updates That Will Not Install Tuesday · Objectives 2.6 and 3.4 — Malware removal and security troubleshooting · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-6-1201
PriorityHigh — client tax files on a possibly infected machine
CategoryMalware
CallerMarisol Diaz, Owner, Diaz Bookkeeping (works from home)
DeviceBusiness laptop · Windows 11 Pro
The Call

"My browser keeps sending me to strange shopping sites, there are pop-ups saying my computer is infected and I should call a number, and Windows Update has failed every day this week. I have my clients' tax files on this laptop. Please tell me they're okay."

You have verified the symptoms yourself during a remote session. The laptop is still connected to Marisol's home network, and she has not called the number in the pop-up.

Stop Here

Marisol wants reassurance. Before the exhibits, write down what you can honestly promise her right now.

Exhibit A — Browser extensions

ExtensionAddedPermissions
Search Defender Plus9 days agoRead and change all your data on all websites
PDF Viewer2 years agoRead data on PDF files

Exhibit B — Windows Security and Windows Update

ItemStatus
Virus & threat protectionReal-time protection is off
Windows UpdateFailed to install — 6 attempts this week
System RestoreOn — 4 restore points from the past 10 days

Exhibit C — Lines added to the hosts file (C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts)

185.220.14.9    www.firstcarolinabank.example
185.220.14.9    login.firstcarolinabank.example

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — follow CompTIA's removal steps in order, and say what you did about the client files. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Verified: browser redirects, fake infection pop-ups with a phone number, real-time protection turned off, and Windows Update failing; a browser extension added 9 days ago with full access to all websites; hosts file redirecting the bank's sites to an outside address.

Removal, in CompTIA's order: quarantined the laptop by disconnecting it from the network; disabled System Restore; remediated — updated the anti-malware definitions using offline files and scanned from the preinstallation environment; removed the extension and the hosts entries. Client files were copied off and scanned separately, and came back clean. Because the infection had disabled protection and blocked updates, recommended reimaging; the client agreed. Scheduled scans and installed all updates; re-enabled System Restore and created a clean restore point.

Educated the user: never call a phone number shown in a pop-up; check browser extensions monthly; change the bank password from a clean device and watch the account.

Escalated: because bank logins may have been redirected, advised her to contact the bank.

Look Back

  1. Return to what you wrote you could promise Marisol. Was it honest? What can a technician never promise about an infected machine?
  2. The pop-up's phone number is the most dangerous thing on the screen. Why is that a social engineering attack rather than a software problem?
  3. This ticket ended in a reimage. At what point did reimaging become the right answer, rather than the easy one?
  4. You get two minutes to teach Marisol one habit. Which one, and how would you say it?
Ticket 2 of 3 Blue Screens After the Update Wednesday · Objective 3.1 — Troubleshooting Windows · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-6-1208
PriorityHigh — a production workstation is unstable
CategoryWindows stability
CallerAndre Wallace, Video Producer, Stonegate Media
DeviceAsset SGM-WS-04 · Workstation · Windows 11 Pro · dedicated graphics card · joined to the domain
The Call

"Since yesterday my workstation blue-screens every time I start a render. It also takes forever to sign in now, and the company apps keep telling me my session is invalid. The clock in the corner is wrong too, but that's probably nothing."

You learn that the graphics driver updated automatically two days ago. Andre's profile follows him between computers in the edit suites.

Stop Here

Andre listed three symptoms. Before the exhibits, write down whether you think there is one cause or several.

Exhibit A — Event Viewer, System log, and Device Manager

Critical   Kernel-Power   41     The system has rebooted without cleanly shutting down first.

                                 The bugcheck was: 0x00000116 (VIDEO_TDR_ERROR)

Device Manager > Display adapters > Driver

Exhibit B — Output of w32tm /query /status

Leap Indicator: 3(not synchronized)
Stratum: 0 (unspecified)
Last Successful Sync Time: unspecified
Source: Local CMOS Clock

The workstation's clock is 7 minutes behind the domain controller.

Exhibit C — Sign-in diagnostics

ItemValue
User sign-in time4 minutes 12 seconds
Roaming profile size38 GB
Largest folder in the profileDesktop — 31 GB of render files

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — three findings, and which ones turned out to be connected. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Reported: blue screens during renders since yesterday; slow sign-in; company apps rejecting his session; clock wrong.

Found: bugcheck 0x116 (VIDEO_TDR_ERROR) after a graphics driver update two days ago. Time service not synchronizing — using the local CMOS clock, 7 minutes behind the domain controller, so sign-in tickets were rejected. A 38 GB roaming profile, 31 GB of it render files on the desktop, causing a 4-minute sign-in.

Action, least disruptive first: rolled back the display driver and paused automatic driver updates for that device until a stable version is confirmed; three test renders completed. Pointed the time service at the domain hierarchy and resynchronized; apps accept his session. Moved render files to project storage; profile 7 GB; sign-in 35 seconds.

Follow-up: CMOS battery to be checked if drift returns. Three causes, not one: the driver, the time service, and the profile size.

Look Back

  1. Return to your "one cause or several" note. Three symptoms arriving together tempt you toward one explanation. How did you resist that, or did you?
  2. You rolled back a driver instead of reimaging. Why is the least disruptive fix the right first move, and when would it stop being right?
  3. Andre called the wrong clock "probably nothing." What did it actually break? What else might users dismiss that matters?
  4. "What changed recently?" found the first cause. Did you ask it for the other two symptoms as well?
Ticket 3 of 3 Remote Help, a Strange Phone, and an AI's Advice Thursday · Objectives 3.3, 4.9, and 4.10 — Mobile security, remote access, and AI · About 20 minutes
Ticket HD-6-1215
PriorityHigh — risky advice about to be acted on
CategoryRemote access · Mobile security · AI
CallerJulie Park, Regional Sales Manager, Coastal Solar (traveling)
DeviceCompany phone; office PC CS-OF-12; personal laptop
The Call

"I'm on the road for two weeks. A coworker says you can get me into my office PC with Remote Desktop if we just open it up on the router. Also, my company phone is hot all the time, the battery dies by noon, and I got a text saying I'm over my data limit — I barely use it. And my brother asked an AI chatbot about my laptop's slow disk, and it gave him a command to run. Should I?"

Coastal Solar provides a company VPN that supports multifactor authentication. Company phones are managed by MDM.

Stop Here

Three pieces of advice from three well-meaning people. Before the exhibits, write down which one worries you most, and why.

Exhibit A — The coworker's suggestion, in an email

Just forward TCP 3389 on the office router to CS-OF-12.
Password is Summer2026! - you'll be in in two minutes.

Exhibit B — Mobile data usage this month, by application

ApplicationData usedPermissions
FlashLight Pro9.4 GBLocation (always), contacts, SMS, background activity
Maps0.3 GBLocation (while in use)
Email0.2 GB—

Exhibit C — What the AI chatbot told Julie's brother

To fix a slow disk, open Command Prompt as administrator and run:
  diskpart
  select disk 0
  clean
Then reinstall Windows for best performance.

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note first — and how you will tell three people that their advice was wrong without making them feel foolish. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution

Remote access: did not forward any ports. Set Julie up on the company VPN with multifactor authentication, then Remote Desktop to CS-OF-12 inside the network. Changed the office PC password, since it had been sent in plain text by email.

Mobile: a flashlight app with "always" location, contacts, SMS, and 9.4 GB of data use is a security issue, not a battery problem. Removed the app through MDM, reviewed remaining permissions, and reported the incident under company policy. Advised Julie to change passwords for accounts used on the phone.

AI advice: the command selects disk 0 and runs clean, which erases its partition information. Not run. Diagnosed the slow disk properly instead and found a nearly full drive.

Communication: thanked the coworker and Julie's brother for trying to help, and explained in one sentence each why their advice would have caused harm.

Look Back

  1. Return to the advice that worried you most. Was it the most dangerous one? How did you decide?
  2. The AI answer was confident, formatted, and wrong. What is your rule for verifying AI-suggested commands, in ten words or fewer?
  3. Exposed Remote Desktop with a weak password is a common way ransomware gets in. How does this ticket connect to what you learned in Units 4 and 5?
  4. Three people gave Julie bad advice because they wanted to help. How do you correct well-meaning people so they still ask you next time?

  • Correcting confident but dangerous advice from coworkers, family, and AI tools
