Safety Before Anything Else

Before you touch a component, you need two habits. Not because the exam tests them — though it does — but because the alternative is destroying equipment in ways nobody can trace back.

Electrostatic Discharge (ESD)

The sudden transfer of static electricity between two objects at different electrical potentials. You need roughly 3,000 volts to feel a static shock and about 20,000 volts to see a spark — but as little as 30 volts can damage a sensitive component. This is the entire problem: the discharge that kills a stick of RAM is one you will never notice.

Damage from ESD comes in two forms. Catastrophic failure kills the component immediately, which is at least honest. Latent damage degrades the component so it fails days or weeks later — and by then nobody connects the failure to the technician who installed it barehanded.

ESD Controls

  • Anti-static wrist strap — clipped to an unpainted metal part of the chassis. This equalizes your potential with the system's. It is the single most effective control.
  • Anti-static mat — for the workbench and, in a shop, the floor.
  • Anti-static bags — components travel and rest in them. Keep the bag; you will need it when the part comes back out.
  • Self-grounding — touching the metal chassis before handling components. This is the fallback when you have no strap, not the preferred method.
  • Humidity — dry air raises static risk. Roughly 40–50% relative humidity is the working target.
  • Handle by the edges — never touch gold contacts, pins, or exposed circuitry.
Never Open a Power Supply

A power supply unit contains capacitors that hold a lethal charge after it is unplugged. There are no user-serviceable parts inside. A PSU is a replaceable unit, full stop. This is the one rule in this course where the consequence of ignoring it is not a broken computer.

A Note on Wrist Straps and Power Supplies

Wrist straps protect components from you. They do not protect you from mains voltage — and wearing one while working near live power is actively dangerous, because it gives current a path to ground through your body. Unplug the system before service.

The Motherboard

The motherboard is the platform everything else plugs into. It carries the CPU socket, memory slots, expansion slots, storage interfaces, power connectors, firmware chip, and the traces that carry signals between all of them.

Form Factors

Form factor defines physical dimensions, mounting hole positions, and the rear I/O panel layout. It determines what fits in which case.

Form FactorTypical SizeExpansion SlotsCommon Use
ATX12 × 9.6 inUp to 7Full-size desktops, workstations, gaming systems
microATX (mATX)9.6 × 9.6 inUp to 4Mainstream office desktops — the most common business build
Mini-ITX6.7 × 6.7 in1Small form factor, home theater, appliances, kiosks

Form Factors Nest Downward

A case built for ATX will almost always accept microATX and Mini-ITX boards, because the mounting holes are a subset and the rear I/O cutout is standardized. The reverse is not true — an ATX board will not fit a Mini-ITX case.

This shows up on the exam as a compatibility question and in the field as the reason you can upgrade a small board into a big case but not the other way around.

Expansion Slots

PCIe (PCI Express) is the current standard. It is a serial, point-to-point interface organized into lanes, and slot width is written as x1, x4, x8, or x16 — the number of lanes available.

  • x16 — graphics cards, high-throughput adapters
  • x8 / x4 — RAID controllers, high-speed network cards, NVMe adapters
  • x1 — sound cards, standard network cards, USB expansion, capture cards

Each PCIe generation roughly doubles the bandwidth per lane over the one before. Generations are backward and forward compatible — a Gen 3 card works in a Gen 4 slot and vice versa, running at the lower of the two speeds.

Physical Size vs. Electrical Lanes

A slot that is physically x16 may only be wired for x4 electrically. The card fits and functions, but at reduced bandwidth. Board documentation lists this; the slot itself does not. This trips up more field technicians than it does exam candidates.

PCI is the legacy parallel standard that PCIe replaced. You still encounter it in older systems and industrial equipment, so it remains on the objectives.

Motherboard Connections and Headers

  • 24-pin ATX main power — primary board power from the PSU
  • 4/8-pin CPU power (EPS) — dedicated processor power, near the socket
  • SATA ports — drive data connections
  • M.2 slots — direct-mount NVMe SSDs and wireless cards
  • Front panel header — power button, reset, power LED, drive activity LED. Individually wired, easy to get wrong, and a classic no-boot cause.
  • USB and audio headers — front panel port connections
  • Fan headers — CPU_FAN, SYS_FAN, often with PWM speed control

Chipsets and Compatibility

The chipset governs which features a board supports: how many USB ports, which PCIe generation, how many SATA connections, whether overclocking is permitted. It also determines processor compatibility alongside the socket.

Intel and AMD use entirely different sockets and chipsets. They are not interchangeable. Server boards distinguish themselves by supporting multiple sockets, far more memory slots, ECC memory, and redundant power.

The Processor

The CPU executes instructions. For the exam, what matters is the characteristics that appear on a spec sheet.

CharacteristicWhat It Means
32-bit vs. 64-bitDetermines addressable memory. 32-bit tops out near 4 GB; 64-bit removes that ceiling in any practical sense. A 64-bit CPU runs a 32-bit OS, but not the reverse.
CoresIndependent processing units on one chip. More cores allow genuine simultaneous execution rather than rapid switching.
MultithreadingOne physical core presenting as two logical processors, improving utilization of idle execution resources.
x86 / x64The instruction set architecture used by Intel and AMD desktop and server processors.
ARMA different architecture emphasizing performance per watt. Dominant in mobile devices and increasingly present in laptops and servers.
Virtualization supportIntel VT-x or AMD-V. Hardware extensions that hypervisors require. Frequently disabled by default in firmware — remember this for Unit 5.

Memory

RAM (Random Access Memory)

Fast, volatile working storage that holds the operating system, running applications, and open data. "Volatile" means contents vanish when power is removed. RAM is the desk you work at; storage is the filing cabinet.

Physical Packages

  • DIMM — Dual Inline Memory Module. Full-length, used in desktops and servers.
  • SO-DIMM — Small Outline DIMM. Shorter, used in laptops, all-in-ones, and small form factor systems.

DDR Generations

DDR (Double Data Rate) SDRAM transfers data on both the rising and falling edge of the clock signal. Each generation increases speed and density while lowering operating voltage.

GenerationNotes
DDR3Legacy. Still found in systems you will be asked to service.
DDR4Very widely deployed. The current baseline in most business fleets.
DDR5Current desktop/laptop generation. Higher bandwidth, higher density, lower voltage.
DDR6Server grade equipment and discrete GPUs. High-end or extreme desktop enthusiasts adopt this memory, when available.
Generations Are Not Interchangeable

DDR3, DDR4, and DDR5 modules use different notch positions specifically so they cannot be installed in the wrong slot. If a module will not seat, stop and check the generation rather than pushing harder. Forcing it damages the slot, and a damaged slot means a new motherboard.

Error Detection and Correction

  • Non-parity — no error checking. Standard consumer and business desktop memory.
  • Parity — detects single-bit errors but cannot correct them.
  • ECC (Error Correcting Code) — detects and corrects single-bit errors on the fly. Requires motherboard and CPU support. Standard in servers, where a silent bit flip is unacceptable.

Multi-Channel Memory

Installing modules in matched pairs or sets lets the memory controller access them in parallel, widening the effective data path. Dual-channel, triple-channel, and quad-channel configurations exist depending on platform.

Slot Color Is a Hint, Not Decoration

Motherboards often alternate slot colors to show which slots pair for multi-channel operation. Two modules in matching colors run dual-channel; the same two modules in adjacent slots may run single-channel and measurably slower. The manual is authoritative.

Power

The power supply unit converts AC from the wall into the DC voltages the system needs, and distributes them over standardized connectors.

Voltage Rails and Connectors

ConnectorSupplies
24-pin ATXMain motherboard power
4-pin / 8-pin EPSDedicated CPU power
6-pin / 8-pin PCIeSupplemental graphics card power
SATA powerDrives and optical devices
MolexLegacy peripherals and some fans

Standard DC rails are +3.3 V, +5 V, and +12 V. The +12 V rail carries the heaviest load, feeding the processor and graphics card.

Specifications That Matter

  • Wattage — total output capacity. Size to actual load with headroom; oversizing modestly is cheap insurance, and undersizing produces intermittent faults that look like everything except a power problem.
  • Input voltage — 115 V versus 230 V. Units with a manual selector switch must match the local supply. Setting one wrong destroys hardware.
  • Efficiency rating — the 80 PLUS tiers (Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Titanium) certify how much input power reaches the components rather than becoming waste heat.
  • Modular cabling — detachable cables so you install only what you use. Improves airflow and serviceability.
  • Redundant supplies — two or more units in a server so one can fail without downtime.

Cooling

Every watt a component consumes becomes heat. Heat raises failure rates and triggers thermal throttling, in which the processor deliberately slows itself to survive. A machine that feels sluggish under load and runs hot is often a cooling problem wearing a performance costume.

  • Passive cooling — heat sinks with no moving parts, relying on surface area and case airflow
  • Active cooling — fans over heat sinks; case fans establishing intake and exhaust
  • Thermal paste — fills microscopic gaps between CPU and heat sink. A thin, even layer. More is not better; excess acts as an insulator.
  • Thermal pads — pre-formed material for memory, VRMs, and M.2 drives
  • Liquid cooling — a pump moves coolant to a radiator. Higher thermal capacity, more components that can fail.
Airflow Has a Direction

Cases are designed for cool air in the front and bottom, warm air out the back and top. A fan installed backward turns a cooling system into a heating system, and nothing about the machine will look wrong. Check the arrow molded into the fan housing.

Firmware: BIOS and UEFI

BIOS / UEFI

Firmware stored on the motherboard that runs the moment power is applied. It initializes and tests hardware, then locates and hands control to a boot device. UEFI is the modern replacement for legacy BIOS, adding support for drives larger than 2 TB, a graphical interface, faster boot, and Secure Boot.

POST

The Power-On Self-Test runs first, verifying that essential hardware is present and responding. When POST fails before video initializes, the system reports through beep codes or a diagnostic LED — patterns whose meanings are defined by the firmware vendor. A machine that beeps is a machine that is telling you something specific.

Common Firmware Settings

  • Boot order — which device the system tries first
  • Secure Boot — permits only signed bootloaders, blocking boot-level malware
  • Virtualization extensions — VT-x / AMD-V, commonly disabled by default
  • USB permissions — enabling or disabling ports, often for security policy
  • Fan and thermal control — curves and monitoring thresholds
  • Passwords — supervisor password for firmware access, user password for boot

TPM and HSM

  • TPM (Trusted Platform Module) — a chip or firmware feature that generates and stores cryptographic keys tied to the specific machine. Full-disk encryption such as BitLocker depends on it.
  • HSM (Hardware Security Module) — a dedicated appliance or card providing key generation and storage at scale, typically for servers and certificate authorities.
The CMOS Battery

A small coin cell — usually a CR2032 — preserves firmware settings and the real-time clock when the system is unplugged. When it dies, the classic symptom is a system that loses its date and time and reverts to default settings on every boot. It is a five-dollar part and one of the most satisfying diagnoses you will make.

The Troubleshooting Methodology

This is the spine of Domain 5, which is 28% of your exam — the largest single domain. Memorize the order.

StepActionIn Practice
1Identify the problemQuestion the user. Determine what changed. Back up before making changes.
2Establish a theory of probable causeQuestion the obvious first. Research symptoms.
3Test the theoryIf confirmed, plan the fix. If not, form a new theory or escalate.
4Establish a plan of actionIdentify potential effects before you act.
5Verify full system functionalityConfirm the fix and implement preventive measures.
6Document findings, actions, and outcomesThe step everyone skips and everyone later wishes they had not.

Why the Order Is the Point

Every step exists because technicians reliably skip it. Swapping parts before identifying the problem is how a bad power supply gets "fixed" with a new motherboard, a new stick of RAM, and four hours of labor. Documenting is how the next person — often you, in six months — avoids repeating the whole diagnosis.

Expect scenario questions that describe a technician doing something out of order and ask what they should have done instead.

Unit 1 Content Recap

  • ESD damage happens well below the threshold you can feel — strap, mat, bag, edges
  • Never open a power supply; the capacitors hold a lethal charge after unplugging
  • Form factor governs fit: ATX, microATX, Mini-ITX, nesting downward into larger cases
  • PCIe lanes come as x1, x4, x8, x16 — and physical width may exceed electrical width
  • RAM is volatile; DIMM for desktops, SO-DIMM for laptops; DDR generations are keyed and not interchangeable
  • ECC detects and corrects; parity only detects; multi-channel needs matched modules in matched slots
  • PSU rails are +3.3 V, +5 V, +12 V; watch wattage, input voltage selection, and 80 PLUS efficiency
  • Thermal paste fills microscopic gaps — thin and even, never generous
  • UEFI replaced legacy BIOS; POST reports pre-video faults through beep codes
  • Six troubleshooting steps, in order, every time — and document at the end
