Why This Exists

Experience alone does not produce learning. Taking a computer apart and putting it back together teaches you comparatively little unless you stop and process what happened — what surprised you, what you predicted wrongly, what you would do differently.

That step is the hinge of real learning from experience, and it is the one adults skip most often because it feels less productive than doing. It is not. Ten minutes of honest reflection consolidates more than another hour at the bench.

The Prompts

Respond to all four, directly in the fields below. Write in prose, in your own voice — this is not a formal paper and it should not read like one.

What a Strong Response Looks Like

"I spent twenty minutes convinced the machine was dead before I realized I had the front panel power switch on the wrong header pins. I had assumed a no-power symptom meant the power supply, because that is what the words suggest. The reading says step one is identify the problem, and I had actually skipped to a theory before gathering information. Next time I am checking every connection I made before I suspect a component I did not touch." That is specific, honest, and connected. It is also about a mistake — most of the best ones are.

Write About What Went Wrong

Reflections describing a week where everything worked are the least useful ones to write and the least interesting to read. Nobody is grading you on competence here. The mistakes are where the learning is, and I would far rather read about the twenty minutes you lost to a header pin than a paragraph telling me the lab went well.
