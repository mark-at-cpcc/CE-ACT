Technical Overview

This section walks through the core concepts for Unit 2 before you hit the full chapter reading in 2.4: storage device types and interfaces, RAID, common peripherals and their connectors, and the four major print technologies with a close look at the laser imaging process.

In Plain Terms

Think of this section as the trailer, not the movie. It gives you the shape of the week so the full reading in 2.4 has something to attach to, instead of hitting you cold.

Storage Devices & Interfaces

HDD (Hard Disk Drive)

Mechanical storage using spinning magnetic platters and a moving read/write head. Slower and more failure-prone than solid-state storage, but cheaper per gigabyte at high capacities.

SSD (Solid State Drive)

Storage with no moving parts, built on flash memory chips. Faster, quieter, and more shock-resistant than an HDD.

NVMe (Non-Volatile Memory Express)

A protocol that lets an SSD communicate directly over the PCIe bus instead of the older SATA interface, cutting latency dramatically. Most NVMe drives use the M.2 form factor.

RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks)

Know These Four Cold

  • RAID 0 — striping. Faster, more storage, zero redundancy. One drive fails, you lose everything.
  • RAID 1 — mirroring. Two drives hold identical data. Full redundancy, no capacity gain.
  • RAID 5 — striping with parity, minimum 3 drives. Survives one drive failure; good balance of speed, capacity, and safety.
  • RAID 10 — a mirrored pair of striped sets, minimum 4 drives. Fast and redundant, but capacity cost is steep.
Memory Hook

RAID 0 = "0 protection." RAID 1 = "1-to-1 mirror." RAID 5 = "5 letters, parity spreads across the drives." If a question describes surviving a single drive failure without doubling your hardware cost, it's almost always RAID 5.

Peripheral Devices

A peripheral is any device connected to a computer that isn't part of the core system — input devices (keyboard, mouse, scanner, webcam), output devices (monitor, speakers), and hybrid devices (touchscreens). Most modern peripherals connect over USB; older systems may still use PS/2, serial, or parallel ports, which you may encounter on legacy equipment.

Installation Pattern to Remember

For most modern peripherals: connect → let the OS detect and install a driver automatically → verify in Device Manager (Windows) or System Settings (macOS) → install manufacturer software only if extra features are needed. If a device isn't recognized, the driver — not the cable — is the first thing to suspect.

The Laser Imaging Process — Seven Steps

  1. Processing — the print job is rendered into a raster image in the printer's memory.
  2. Charging — a charging roller applies a uniform negative charge to the drum.
  3. Exposing — a laser selectively discharges areas of the drum to "write" the image.
  4. Developing — toner clings to the discharged areas of the drum.
  5. Transferring — a transfer roller charges the paper so toner moves from drum to page.
  6. Fusing — heat and pressure melt the toner permanently onto the paper.
  7. Cleaning — excess toner is removed from the drum before the cycle repeats.
Common Exam Trap

Students often reverse Charging and Exposing, or mix up Transferring and Fusing. Anchor it this way: charge first (blank canvas), expose second (draw the image), develop third (toner sticks to the drawing), transfer fourth (move it to paper), fuse fifth (make it permanent).
